IB DP Assessment Overview

English A Literature HL is assessed through four components, both external and internal, requiring close reading, extended writing, and oral performance.

📝

Paper 1: Literary Commentary

Two unseen literary extracts (prose and poetry). HL students write detailed commentaries on both. No notes allowed.

External · 35%
✍️

Paper 2: Comparative Essay

A 1 hour 45 minute essay comparing two works studied in the course. Four questions are set, and you choose one. Argues a claim with textual evidence.

External · 25%
📖

HL Essay

A 1,200–1,500 word formal essay on a literary work studied in the course. Independently researched, written outside class.

External · 20%
🎤

Individual Oral (IO)

A 10-minute oral on an extract from each of two texts studied, exploring how a global issue is reflected through literary craft.

Internal · 20%
Critical Lenses

Approaches to the Text

DP Literature requires you to apply theoretical frameworks as analytical tools, using them to generate insight.

01

Dramaturgical Theory (Erving Goffman)

All social life is performance. Analyse front/back stage, impression management, and performance breakdown in texts. Essential for Master Harold.

02

Postcolonial Theory (Frantz Fanon)

The Manichaean division of the colonial world into settler and native zones. How texts represent space, bodies, language, and the psychology of the colonised. Key for Master Harold.

03

The Master-Slave Dialectic (G.W.F. Hegel)

Two consciousnesses in struggle for recognition. The master requires the slave's recognition, which is worthless; genuine freedom is impossible in this structure.

04

Psychoanalytic Reading (Sigmund Freud)

Displacement, the unconscious, repression. Characters' stated motivations are not their real ones. What is the text not saying?

05

Reader-Response Theory

Meaning is created in the encounter between text and reader. How does the text position the reader? Who is the implied audience, and what assumptions does it make?

06

Late Modernism & Literary Form

How does form (structure, narrative voice, time, fragmentation) carry meaning? Late modernist texts interrogate the very tools of representation.

What Each Criterion Demands

  • A · Read between the lines: what is implied, not just stated? Draw reasoned conclusions from implications.
  • B · Name authorial choices and evaluate their effectiveness. How do they shape meaning?
  • C · Use the guiding question's exact language in thesis + every topic sentence.
  • D · Formal register, precise vocabulary, no contractions, no "I think."
Step-by-Step Commentary Approach
1

Read & annotate (6 min per text)

Read twice. First read: understand the content and situation. Second read: mark structural shifts, tonal changes, repeated images, and the guiding question's key terms. Identify the text type immediately: prose, poetry, and drama each demand different analytical vocabulary.

2

Identify your central argument (4 min)

Answer the guiding question in one sentence; that sentence is your thesis. You need one central interpretive claim, not a list of features. Example: "Through accumulating imagery of paralysis and confinement, Wharton constructs Ethan's environment as an extension of his psychological imprisonment."

3

Introduction: establish your argument

State: author + title + genre/form + approximate period. Answer the guiding question directly with your thesis. Briefly identify the structural principles you will analyse. Do not retell the plot or summarise the extract.

4

Body paragraphs: Technique → Effect → Evaluate → Link to GQ

  • Name the technique precisely: which metaphor is it? What tenor/vehicle? What connotations?
  • Quote briefly and exactly; analyse the specific words, not the general idea
  • State the effect on the reader: what does the reader feel / think / understand?
  • Evaluate effectiveness: why this word? What does the author "effectively" / "powerfully" achieve?
  • Link back to the guiding question language (exact words or close synonyms)
5

Form & structure: analyse these in every commentary

Prose fiction: narrative voice (1st/3rd limited/omniscient), tense, sentence length, free indirect discourse, paragraph rhythm, temporal order.
Poetry: lineation, stanza form, enjambment, caesura, rhyme scheme, meter, volta, speaker vs. poet.
Drama: stage directions as secondary text, dialogue rhythms, stichomythia, silence/pause, proxemics, status shifts encoded in form of address.

6

Conclusion: widen the frame

Return to the guiding question and your central claim. Go beyond the extract: what does it ultimately reveal about the human condition, power, identity, or language itself? Avoid merely restating your introduction. Zoom out to broader significance.

Top 10 Tips for Paper 1
01Read and annotate carefully before writing anything▼

This is a reading test first. Annotate in the margins: big ideas, authorial choices, GQ keywords. Students who annotate carefully almost always outperform those who dive straight into writing.

02Focus on the Guiding Question and use its exact language▼

The GQ tells you what to analyse. Use its exact words (or synonyms) in your thesis, every topic sentence, and your conclusion. Examiners actively look for this focus when marking Criterion C.

03Plan before you write, even a rough outline▼

Spend 4 minutes: write your thesis + 4 paragraph topic words. A clear plan prevents wandering and saves time. Aim for 5 focused body paragraphs, not 2 enormous ones.

04Discuss implications, not just literal meaning▼

Your job is to interpret and analyse, not retell. Read between the lines: what is implied but not stated? What does the text assume about its reader? Discussing implications is how you reach the highest band for Criterion A.

05Name authorial choices precisely and evaluate their effect▼

Which metaphor is it? What two things are being compared and why? Every technique must connect to an effect on the reader and a meaning. This is Criterion B.

06Analyse form and structure, not only content▼

Prose: sentence length, rhythm, narrative voice. Poetry: lineation, enjambment, caesura, stanza structure. Drama: stage directions, silence, status shifts. Form carries meaning; never ignore it.

07Go beyond the obvious and explore subtleties▼

Think about what most students will write, then go further. What is the author indirectly suggesting? What does the text assume about its audience? Nuanced readings earn top marks for Criterion A.

08Avoid the "firehose": fewer techniques, deeper analysis▼

Listing 15 techniques with one sentence each scores poorly. Choose 4 per paragraph and analyse each fully: name → quote → effect → evaluate → link to GQ.

09Write a proper conclusion and don't run out of time▼

Reserve 5 minutes. Zoom out: what does this extract ultimately reveal about the human condition, language, or power? Don't just restate the introduction. End with a flourish.

10Maintain formal academic register throughout▼

No contractions, no "I think," no "the text is talking about." Use the author as subject: "Conrad constructs…", "Fugard positions…". Academic register signals Criterion D competence from sentence one.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid
Ignoring the Guiding Question
Put GQ keywords in your thesis and every topic sentence.
Writing without a plan
Take 4 minutes to draft paragraph topics before writing a sentence.
Summarising the content
Use the author as subject; focus on craft decisions, not plot retelling.
The "firehose" of techniques
Every technique must connect to effect and meaning. Fewer, deeper is better.
Neglecting form and structure
Lineation, syntax and stage directions all carry meaning. Always discuss form.
Skipping the conclusion
Reserve 5 min. Widen the frame instead of restating your introduction.

Breaking Down the Question
"Literature brings into the open important issues of the time." How have authors presented "important issues" in two works you have studied?
"brings into the open"
Makes public; suggests literature can examine difficult topics, reveal what is hidden or taboo
"important issues"
Things that matter to society: themes and messages conveyed to an audience
"of the time"
Issues important when the work was written AND/OR when the action takes place; consider historical context
"presented"
HOW: characters, actions, events, literary and dramatic techniques, narrative choices
Sample Practice Questions
"Literature brings into the open important issues of the time." How have authors presented "important issues" in two works you have studied?
What are the central social/political/moral issues? How does each author make them visible through literary craft?
Discuss how two literary works you have studied portray the struggle to be understood.
Who struggles to be understood? By whom? How do the authors use technique (silence, dialogue, narrative voice) to show this struggle?
Some literary texts, although set in a particular place or time, convey ideas that are universal. In what ways is this true in two of the works you have studied?
What is universal? How do specific historical settings open onto broader human truths? Compare the methods, not just the themes.
Discuss how two works you have studied present concepts of good and bad, not as absolute notions, but as a matter of individual perception.
Are good/evil relative in these texts? How do authors use character, irony, or perspective to destabilise moral absolutes?
Thesis Formula

A strong Paper 2 thesis names both works, identifies the central link, and signals the comparative argument.

[Author A] in [Title A]
+
[Author B] in [Title B]
both
[shared concern / theme]
yet
their methods diverge: where [A]… , [B]…

Sample Thesis (Master Harold + Death of a Salesman)

  • Both Fugard and Miller build a play around a man who has been taught to measure himself by a system that will not measure him back, but their methods diverge: Fugard keeps his three characters inside one room and one unbroken afternoon, so that the hierarchy is enacted in real time between people who love each other, whereas Miller dissolves the walls of the Loman house and lets the past walk through them, so that the damage arrives as memory rather than as event.
Essay Structure Framework
↑

Introduction: state both works and your comparative thesis

Name both authors and titles. Answer the question with a precise comparative thesis. Name the critical lens or analytical framework you will apply. Do not retell plots. Aim for four focused sentences.

P1

First comparative point

Choose one literary technique or thematic argument, then ask how each author handles it. Quote both texts in the same paragraph. Analyse differences and similarities together, not separately. Topic sentence must use the question's language and name both texts.

P2

Second comparative point: develop or complicate

Develop your first point or introduce a new technique. Show a deeper layer: where the texts seem similar on the surface but diverge in method or meaning. Use comparative language to make the relationship explicit.

P3

Third comparative point: the most analytical

Apply your critical lens explicitly: "Through a Fanonian lens, both Conrad and Fugard…" or "Applying Goffman's concept of front/back stage…" Connect technique to ideology, psychology, or power. This paragraph should feel like the climax of your argument.

↓

Conclusion: the comparison itself is your subject

Return to your thesis. What does comparing these two works reveal that reading each alone would not? The comparison itself should be your conclusion's subject: what it shows about literature, power, language, or the human condition.

What Examiners Look For: Key Reminders
Criterion A: Knowledge
Context matters less than showing deep understanding of the works through specific, precise references.
Criterion B: Analysis
Compare HOW authors make choices, not just what the texts are about. Use the author as subject of every analytical sentence.
Criterion C: Focus
Every paragraph must be balanced, giving equal attention to both works, and must use the question's exact language.
Criterion D: Language
No contractions and no first person. Aim for varied vocabulary, accurate grammar and precise terminology. Read your work back once.

The Golden Rule of Paper 2

  • Never alternate: one paragraph on Text A, then one on Text B. That produces a summary of two texts presented side by side.
  • Genuine comparison = both texts appear in the SAME paragraph, around the SAME point, analysed simultaneously.
  • Wear your "comparative hat" from the first sentence to the last.

What Makes a Good Global Issue?

  • Transnational: relevant beyond one country or culture
  • Significant: it has real-world consequences for real people
  • Genuinely embedded in the literary text (not grafted on)
  • Specific enough to generate close analysis, such as "how language performs racial hierarchy"

Classroom workshops and collaborative exercises used in our DP Literature lessons. These activities build close-reading habits, discussion skills, and analytical confidence.

Workshops: "Master Harold" & Poetry
Collaborative

Sharing Lines

Oral & Close-Reading Activity

Students receive individual lines from a poem or dramatic text. Working without seeing the whole, each person reads their line aloud in sequence, and the class reconstructs meaning collaboratively, noticing how each fragment contributes to the whole. Builds sensitivity to enjambment, tone, and the cumulative effect of language.

  • Focus: how individual lines gain meaning in context
  • DP link: Poetry analysis (lineation and enjambment)
  • Skill: active listening, close reading, oral fluency
Objects

Activity with Objects

Symbolism & Theme Exploration

Physical objects are used as entry points into a literary text. Students select or are given an object (a bench, a coat, a kite) and explore what it means literally, then what it might symbolise. Particularly powerful for Master Harold, where the bench, the waiter's coat and the kite are all loaded with symbolic weight before the play is read closely.

  • Focus: symbol vs. literal object; surface vs. deep meaning
  • DP link: symbolism, motif, literary devices
  • Skill: inferential thinking, thematic connection-making
Poetry

Poetry Word Association

Pre-Reading & Vocabulary Activity

Before reading a poem closely, students free-associate with key words from the text, writing down whatever connections, images, or feelings those words trigger. This activates prior knowledge and personal resonance, making the encounter with the poem richer. Also reveals the range of connotations a single word carries across different readers.

  • Focus: connotation, reader-response, personal lens
  • DP link: Reader-Response Theory; Paper 1 Criterion A
  • Skill: lateral thinking, vocabulary sensitivity
Small Group

Small Group Activity: Poetry or Prose

Analytical Discussion Exercise

A structured small-group discussion framework for any poem or prose extract. Groups are assigned specific analytical tasks: one group focuses on imagery and diction, another on structure and form, a third on tone and speaker. Groups then share findings with the class, building a collaborative commentary. Mirrors the demands of Paper 1.

  • Focus: distribution of analytical tasks across a text
  • DP link: Paper 1 (technique, effect, meaning)
  • Skill: collaboration, oral articulation, structured analysis
Visual Art

Using Art with Poetry

Ekphrasis & Visual Interpretation

A painting or artwork is presented alongside a poem. Students first analyse the visual image (composition, colour, subject, mood), then read the poem to explore how the two works speak to each other. Develops the ability to read across media, and sharpens vocabulary for describing atmosphere, perspective, and form.

  • Focus: ekphrasis; cross-media interpretation
  • DP link: IO global issues; reader-response; visual literacy
  • Skill: observation, comparative analysis, descriptive language
★ Master Harold

Daisy and Karl Narrative

Creative Writing Response

A creative writing exercise inspired by the structure and themes of Master Harold. Students write a short dramatic narrative between two characters (named "Daisy" and "Karl") placed in an unequal power relationship, exploring how dialogue, stage directions, and silence can carry themes of race, class, or family without stating them directly. The exercise reveals how much is said through what is not said.

  • Focus: how dramatic form carries theme; subtext in dialogue
  • DP link: HL Essay creative response; Paper 1 drama analysis
  • Skill: dramatic writing, implicit meaning, empathic imagination
