Antigone   Sophocles · c. 441 BCE · Drama

A woman buries her brother against an edict, and the ruler who forbade it loses everything he was protecting.

Snapshot

LengthRoughly 1,350 lines. Performable in about 90 minutes. You can hold the whole shape in your head, which is why it is an unusually forgiving work to plan under exam pressure.
Time coveredA single day, dawn to nightfall, in Thebes, immediately after the assault of the Seven.
CastAntigone, Ismene, Kreon, Haimon, Teiresias, Eurydice, a Guard, a Messenger, Chorus of Theban elders.
Form facts to ownPrologue, parodos, five episodes each closed by a stasimon (choral ode), exodos. Stichomythia for confrontation, kommos for shared lament, messenger speech for offstage violence.
Original conditionsDaytime open-air performance at the City Dionysia, masked male actors, three speaking actors maximum, a civic audience of thousands who were also the voting citizens of the polis.
The translation problemYou are analysing a translator's English, not Sophocles' Greek. This changes what you are allowed to claim. See the translation protocol below.

Translation protocol

You are studying this work in translation. What you may claim about it is therefore narrower than for the works written in English, and examiners notice when a candidate forgets this.

  • Name your translator in the essay, once. Fitts and Fitzgerald, Wyckoff, Grene, Fagles, Heaney, Lloyd-Jones and Anne Carson produce noticeably different plays.
  • You may analyse: structure, sequence, entrances and exits, who speaks and for how long, silences, imagery patterns that survive translation, the shape of arguments, dramatic irony, the placement of odes.
  • You should be careful with: diction, register, sound patterning, rhythm, word order, puns. If you discuss these, attribute them to the translator's choice rather than to Sophocles.
  • A high-value move: where the Greek is famously ambiguous, such as deinon at line 332, note that translators diverge and build your reading on your edition's choice. This converts a limitation into evidence of critical awareness and reads at once as a top-band gesture to the examiner.
  • Never write that Sophocles 'chose the word' unless you are reading Greek. Write that your translation renders it as x, which foregrounds y.

Structure map

SectionWhat happensWhy it matters
Prologue (1 to 99)Antigone tells Ismene of the edict and her intention; Ismene refuses.The play opens on two sisters alone before the city is awake. The decision is made before the ruler has spoken a word on stage. Sophocles gives the audience the private, female, nocturnal world first, so that Kreon's public daylight entrance reads as an interruption.
Parodos (100 to 161)Chorus celebrates the defeat of Argos and the dawn.Triumphalist, relieved, civic. The elders are not neutral observers; they are the city that has just been saved, and that gratitude is exactly why they will defer to Kreon for far too long.
Episode 1 (162 to 331)Kreon's inaugural speech and edict; the Guard reports the first, mysterious burial.Kreon speaks in the language of a political manifesto. The first burial is never explained, which some readers take as divine and others as Antigone's first attempt. Ambiguity you can use.
Stasimon 1 (332 to 375)Ode on Man.The most quoted passage in Greek tragedy and the ideological hinge of the play. Man crosses seas, tames the earth, invents speech and law, and is helpless only before death. Placed immediately after a burial nobody can explain.
Episode 2 (376 to 581)Antigone caught; the confrontation with Kreon; Ismene tries to share the guilt.The agon proper. Watch the metre of the exchange tighten into single-line stichomythia as the argument becomes a collision rather than a debate.
Stasimon 2 (582 to 625)Ode on the doom of the house of Labdakos.Shifts the frame from politics to inherited curse. Sophocles keeps changing the lens on the same event so that no single reading exhausts it.
Episode 3 (626 to 780)Haimon and Kreon; father and son destroy each other's arguments.The political argument is restaged as a family argument. Haimon introduces the decisive information that the city privately agrees with Antigone, which means Kreon's claim to speak for the city has already collapsed.
Stasimon 3 (781 to 800)Ode to Eros.Short, and easy to skip. Do not. It reframes everything that follows as driven by desire rather than principle, and it is the Chorus's first open sympathy with Antigone.
Episode 4 (801 to 943)Antigone's kommos and final speech; she is led to the tomb.Her only lyric passage. She stops arguing and starts mourning. The contested brother argument sits here, and this is where most strong essays find their best material.
Stasimon 4 (944 to 987)Ode of mythic parallels: Danae, Lycurgus, Cleopatra's sons.Three imprisonments. The Chorus is trying to console by analogy and every analogy is disquieting.
Episode 5 (988 to 1114)Teiresias; the prophecy; Kreon yields.Kreon accuses the prophet of corruption, the reflex of a man who can no longer imagine disinterested opposition. Then he yields, in the wrong order: he goes to the body before the tomb.
Stasimon 5 (1115 to 1154)Hymn to Dionysos.Ecstatic, hopeful, and immediately falsified. Sophocles times the audience's relief to maximise the drop.
Exodos (1155 to 1353)Messenger speech; Eurydice's silent exit; Kreon returns with Haimon's body; Eurydice's death reported; Kreon's lament; choral coda.Three deaths, none of them on stage, all of them reported. The play refuses spectacle at exactly the moment a lesser play would supply it.

Check your edition's word and build your reading on it. Stasimon 1, 365 to 375 (Chorus)Skilled beyond hope in the devices of art, he moves now to evil, now to good.The ode's turn. The Chorus has just described Antigone and Kreon at once without knowing it.Locked
Episode 2, 441 to 443 (Kreon / Antigone)Do you admit this, or deny it? I say I did it. I do not deny it.The single most efficient moment of characterisation in the play. No mitigation, no plea, no rhetoric. Antigone's refusal to defend herself in Kreon's terms is what makes her ungovernable.Locked
Episode 2, 450 to 457 (Antigone)It was not Zeus who made this proclamation, nor Justice who dwells with the gods below. Episode 2, 472 to 476 (Chorus/Kreon)The stiffest iron, baked hard in the fire, is the first to crack and shatter.Kreon's image for Antigone's rigidity. It is accurate about her and blind about himself, which is the definition of dramatic irony in a play with no gods on stage.Locked
Episode 2, 523 (Antigone)I was born to share in love, not in hatred.The most quoted line in the play, and the most abused. It is a reply to Kreon's insistence that enemy and friend must remain distinct even in death. It is a statement about the reach of obligation, not a sentimental declaration. Episode 2, 525 (Kreon)Then go below and love them, if you must love. While I live, no woman shall rule.The political argument becomes a gender argument in a single line. Sophocles marks the moment Kreon's principle acquires a personal motive, and from here his reasoning deteriorates.Locked
Episode 3, 672 to 676 (Kreon)There is no greater evil than anarchy. It destroys cities, it overturns houses.Kreon's deepest fear, stated plainly. Note the sequence: cities first, houses second. By the end, his house is destroyed and the city is intact, which is the play's answer to him.Locked
Episode 3, 707 to 709 (Haimon)Whoever thinks that he alone is wise, that he alone has speech and judgement, open him up and you will find him empty.The argument that should have worked. Haimon offers Kreon a dignified exit and Kreon refuses it. Tragedy in Sophocles is frequently a matter of refused off-ramps.Locked
Episode 3, 733 to 739 (Kreon / Haimon)Should the city tell me how I am to rule? Is not the city held to be the ruler's?Stichomythia at its sharpest: one line each, question against question. In two exchanges Kreon states the tyrannical position explicitly and loses the audience.Locked
Episode 3, 751 (Haimon)Then she shall die, and dying destroy another.Deliberately ambiguous: Kreon hears a threat against himself, the audience hears a suicide. Sophocles builds the catastrophe out of a misheard line.Locked
Stasimon 3, 781 to 790 (Chorus)Eros, unconquered in battle, you who fall upon possessions.Twenty lines that reclassify the play. After two episodes of political argument the Chorus decides the cause is desire. This is also the first time the elders are audibly on the side of the young.Locked
Episode 4, 806 to 816 (Antigone)No wedding-song was sung for me. I go to be the bride of Acheron.Her switch from iambic argument to lyric lament. The marriage-to-death imagery is sustained and will be literalised when Haimon dies embracing her. Quote this for any question about how a work treats death, or ritual, or the unfulfilled life.Locked
Episode 4, 823 to 838 (Antigone / Chorus)I heard of the most pitiable death of the Phrygian stranger, Niobe. You are mortal, and mortal-born. It is a great thing for a mortal to share the fate of a god.Antigone compares herself to Niobe; the Chorus corrects her, gently and devastatingly. She is not permitted even the consolation of her own analogy. Some readers find the Chorus cruel here, others tender. Say which and why.Locked
Episode 4, 891 to 894 (Antigone)Tomb, bridal chamber, deep-dug home, forever guarded.Three nouns for one space. The tomb is simultaneously grave, marriage bed and house, and the accumulation enacts the collapse of every future she might have had.Locked
Episode 4, 905 to 912 (Antigone)A husband lost, another could be found; a child, another could be borne. But with my mother and father in Hades, no brother could ever bloom for me again.The contested passage. Goethe wished it away; Aristotle may have known it; some editors have judged it an interpolation. It reasons like an accountant about a sacrifice she has already made on absolute grounds. Take a position: either it exposes that her principle was always particular rather than universal, or it is a grief-stricken mind reaching for any justification at the point of death. Examiners reward candidates who know the passage is contested and argue anyway.Locked
Episode 4, 921 to 928 (Antigone)What law of the gods have I transgressed? Why should I look to the gods any longer?Her faith fails before her resolve does. The play does not reward her piety within the world of the play; vindication arrives only after she is dead, and she never hears it.Locked
Episode 5, 1023 to 1027 (Teiresias)All men make mistakes. The man who errs is not unwise or unblessed if, having fallen into trouble, he seeks a cure and does not remain unmoved.The play's stated ethic, and it belongs to neither protagonist. Sophocles gives the reasonable position to a blind outsider whom Kreon immediately accuses of corruption.Locked
Episode 5, 1033 to 1047 (Kreon)The whole tribe of prophets loves silver.The tyrant's last defence: if the opposition cannot be answered it must be bought. Sophocles shows the collapse of a mind through its explanations rather than through its actions.Locked
Episode 5, 1068 to 1073 (Teiresias)You have thrust below one who belongs above, lodging a living soul in a tomb, and you keep here a corpse that belongs to the gods below.The exact formulation of Kreon's crime: he has inverted the categories, not merely broken a custom. This single line converts a political dispute into a cosmological one and is the most examiner-friendly quotation in the last third of the play.Locked
Episode 5, 1113 to 1114 (Kreon)I fear it is best to keep the established laws to the end of one's life.The reversal, expressed with no self-understanding at all. He yields to prophecy, not to argument, and he still calls it law. Then he goes to bury Polyneikes before releasing Antigone, and the order of those two acts kills his son.Locked
Exodos, 1234 to 1241 (Messenger)He clasped her in his failing arms, and gasping out a sharp stream of blood upon her white cheek.Offstage violence, reported. Sophocles will not stage the deaths, and the messenger speech gives him a narrative register the dialogue cannot reach. The marriage imagery Antigone began is completed here in blood.Locked
Exodos, 1244 to 1256 (Messenger / Chorus)She turned and went in silence.Eurydice hears of her son's death and leaves without a word. Her silence is the most discussed stage moment in the play, and it is a choice available only in drama: a character who refuses her own scene.Locked
Exodos, 1317 to 1325 (Kreon)I am no more than nothing. Lead me away, a rash and useless man, who killed you, my son, and you, my wife.Kreon survives, which is worse. He gets the recognition, the lament and the last long speech; Antigone does not. Any essay claiming the play endorses Antigone has to explain why Sophocles gives its ending to him.Locked
Exodos, 1347 to 1353 (Chorus)Wisdom is by far the chief part of happiness. Great words of the arrogant pay their price in great blows, and teach wisdom in old age.The choral coda: general, sententious, and somewhat inadequate to what has just happened. Decide whether Sophocles endorses it or exposes it. Strong candidates note that the moral fits Kreon and not Antigone, and that the play therefore ends by describing only half of itself.Locked

The seven concepts

IdentityAntigone's identity is entirely relational: sister, daughter of Oedipus, bride of Hades. She never describes herself in isolation. Kreon's identity is positional: he becomes tyrannical at the moment he confuses the office with the man.
CultureBurial rite is the culture under dispute. The play asks whether a custom older than the state can be overruled by the state, and answers by destroying the man who tried.
CreativitySophocles works under severe constraints, three actors, no stage violence, a fixed festival form, and converts every constraint into meaning.
CommunicationThe play is a sequence of failed conversations. Stichomythia is the form of communication breaking down into collision, and Eurydice's silent exit is the endpoint.
PerspectiveFive perspectives are given full statement, Antigone, Kreon, Ismene, Haimon, Teiresias, and the Chorus keeps changing which one it holds. No character is granted the play's authority.
TransformationKreon is transformed and survives; Antigone is not transformed and dies. Reversal in this play is punishment rather than growth.
RepresentationThebes is Athens' laboratory: a city in which Athenian audiences could watch political questions play out in a place that was safely not Athens.

Authorial choices toolkit

TechniqueWhere to find itWhat it achieves
StichomythiaEpisode 2 with Antigone, Episode 3 with Haimon, Episode 5 with TeiresiasSingle alternating lines. Acceleration into collision. Track where it starts in each scene and you can show Sophocles calibrating the temperature of an argument.
The choral ode as interruptionAll five stasimaEach ode changes the frame rather than summarising the episode. The Ode on Man follows an unexplained burial; the Ode to Eros follows a political quarrel. The gap between episode and ode is where the meaning is.
The kommosEpisode 4, 806 to 882Sung lament shared between actor and Chorus. Antigone's only lyric register. A change of metre is a change of the audience's relationship to her.
The messenger speechExodos, 1155 onwardLong narrative in the mouth of a minor character. Allows spectacle that cannot be staged, and imposes a pause between event and reaction.
The agonEpisodes 2 and 3Formal debate structure inherited from the law court and the assembly. Athenian audiences heard these as recognisable civic speech.
Entrances and exitsIsmene 99, Eurydice 1256, Antigone 943Three characters leave and never return. In performance the empty space they leave is an effect. Ismene simply vanishes from the play, which is either carelessness or the play's judgement on compliance.
Dramatic irony without gods on stageThroughoutNo deity appears. Teiresias is the only channel, and he arrives late. Everything the audience knows in advance about the myth is doing work that the text never states.
Imagery of ships, yokes, birds, earthKreon 189 and 477, Chorus 332 to 375, Teiresias 1080Kreon's images are of control and breaking-in; the Chorus's are of mastery over nature; Teiresias returns birds and dogs as polluted and uncontrollable. Track one image family across the play and you have a whole paragraph.

Context that earns its place

The factUse it forDo not
Produced around 441 BCE in Periclean Athens, a radical democracy about fifteen years after the Persian wars.Kreon's edict would have sounded to that audience like the language of a man claiming more authority than any Athenian officeholder was permitted.Do not write that Sophocles was attacking Pericles, or that the play is about democracy versus tyranny. It is about the limits of legitimate authority, which is a narrower and better claim.
Burial of kin was a religious obligation enforced by custom and, in Athens, by law. An unburied corpse polluted the city.This makes Kreon's edict not merely harsh but technically dangerous to the polis, which is precisely what Teiresias reports.Do not present Antigone's act as private sentiment. It is a public religious duty, which is why the city privately agrees with her.
Performed at the City Dionysia, a civic and religious festival, before an audience of citizens, masked, in daylight, with three speaking actors.The three-actor rule explains why Ismene and Eurydice cannot be on stage together with others, and why so much is reported.Do not claim to know how the original audience felt. Say what the conditions made possible or impossible.
Thebes in Athenian tragedy functions as the anti-Athens, a city where political disaster can be staged at a safe distance.Useful for any question about setting or place: the setting is chosen for what it licenses, not for its realism.Avoid treating Thebes as a real historical location with documented customs.
The play precedes Oedipus the King in composition but follows it in story order.You can note that the audience knew the myth, so suspense is not the source of tension; inevitability is.Do not treat the three Theban plays as a trilogy. They were written years apart for different festivals.
Twentieth-century afterlife: Anouilh's 1944 Antigone staged in occupied Paris, Brecht's 1948 version, Heaney's The Burial at Thebes in 2004, Kamila Shamsie's Home Fire in 2017.Excellent for a question about how works are reinterpreted over time, and evidence that the play's political charge is live.Only use an adaptation if you can say something precise about it. A list of titles earns nothing.

Critical voices

Hegel

The tragedy is not right against wrong but two goods in collision, family piety and the state, each of which becomes destructive by claiming to be the whole.

The most useful frame for a balanced essay, but say Hegel's reading rather than presenting it as the play's meaning. Then complicate it: Hegel's symmetry is disturbed by the fact that only one of the two is granted a lament.

George Steiner, Antigones

The play stages five permanent oppositions: men and women, age and youth, society and the individual, the living and the dead, mortals and gods.

A ready-made structure for a paragraph plan. Choose two oppositions rather than listing all five.

Martha Nussbaum, The Fragility of Goodness

Both Antigone and Kreon simplify. Each avoids tragic conflict by recognising only one kind of value, and the Chorus's flexibility is the play's positive standard.

The best corrective to essays that make Antigone a straightforward heroine. Very strong for questions about moral complexity.

Judith Butler, Antigone's Claim

Antigone does not stand outside the state but speaks in the very language of sovereignty she defies, and her kinship is already deformed by incest.

Use for questions about language, power, or the family. Handle briefly and precisely; a half-understood Butler reference costs more than it earns.

Bonnie Honig, Antigone Interrupted

Mourning is a political act and the play is about who is permitted to grieve publicly.

The strongest frame available for questions about mourning, ritual and public grief, and a natural bridge to a partner work later.

Jean Anouilh, 1944 adaptation

Antigone as refusal without a cause; Creon as the exhausted administrator who says yes to keep the trains running.

Use to show the play's meanings are historically mobile. Note that Anouilh's version passed German censorship, which is itself an argument about the text's ambiguity.

The Antigone-only version you can write today

Moves one to three, single work

Claim. Kreon's authority fails not because it is opposed but because he cannot separate the office from the man.

Located reference. His inaugural speech builds legitimacy from the ship-of-state image, a figure of collective survival that licenses absolute command; two hundred lines later he answers Antigone with the assertion that while he lives no woman shall rule.

Authorial choice. Sophocles gives the political principle and the personal grievance to the same man in the same idiom, so that the audience hears an argument degrade into a grudge without Kreon noticing. The deterioration is staged through diction rather than announced by the Chorus, which is why the Chorus can still defer to him afterwards.

Written this way, the paragraph needs one further sentence and a second work to become a Paper 2 paragraph. Written as plot summary, it needs to be thrown away.

Comparative connectives, and the sentence that has to follow each one, unlock with the partner work.

Criterion B2 calibration

What moves a comparative analysis mark from 3 to 4 to 5 unlocks with the partner work.

What to do meanwhile. Write single-work paragraphs on Antigone that follow moves one to three of the machine: a claim, a short located reference, and the authorial choice behind it. A paragraph built that way takes a second work without being rewritten. A paragraph that retells the plot never will.

Question bank and unpacking

Paper 2 questions are general by design: they are written to be answerable on any two works from any Language A course. That generality is the trap. A question about power is not a question about power in general, and the first thing a strong answer does is decide which kind of power it is about. Use these now as single-work planning practice on Antigone.

Unpacking the central term

If the question saysIt could mean any ofWhat to do
PowerPolitical, institutional, physical, economic, sexual, epistemic (who decides what counts as knowledge), linguistic (who controls definitions), moral authority.Choose the two kinds your works actually stage, and say so in the thesis. An essay on 'power' in general is an essay on nothing.
The pastInherited guilt, historical trauma, personal memory, family curse, nostalgia, unfinished mourning, documentary record.Decide whether the past in your works is recalled, inherited, or physically present. That distinction is the whole essay.
ConflictBetween characters, between value systems, within a character, between a character and a community, between form and content.Take the least obvious of these that your works support. Internal conflict and formal conflict are where the marks are.
SettingPhysical location, historical moment, social world, theatrical space, imagined geography, the domestic interior.For drama, always include theatrical space. For fiction, include the order in which places are revealed.
SympathySympathy created, withdrawn, withheld, manipulated, or demanded and refused.The strongest essays track a change in sympathy across the work rather than asserting a fixed level of it.
EndingsLast line, last scene, resolution, refusal of resolution, who survives, who speaks, what the work declines to settle.Answer at least two of these. An ending is not only the final sentence.
FreedomLegal, political, economic, psychological, the freedom to choose one's death, freedom from the past.Distinguish being freed from being free. Antigone is free to choose the manner of her death and free in nothing else.
SilenceEnforced silence, chosen silence, the unsayable, what a form cannot show, what a period would not permit.Pair one instance of a character's silence with one instance of the work's own refusal to narrate.

The criteria, and what moves each mark

The official band descriptors are in the subject guide and your teacher will give them to you. What follows is not the descriptor wording; it is a practical account of what separates one mark from the next, and what to do about it.

Criterion A · Knowledge, understanding and interpretation · 5 marks

What it rewards

  • Knowledge deployed in service of an argument, never displayed for its own sake
  • Interpretation: a claim about meaning that someone could disagree with
  • References that are precise, short and chosen because they prove the point
  • Implications drawn out, so the essay says what follows from the evidence

What moves the mark

FromWhat has to change
1 to 2Stop retelling. If a sentence could be written by someone who has read a summary, cut it.
2 to 3Attach every reference to a claim. No quotation appears without a reason for being there.
3 to 4Make the claims contestable. 'Antigone values family' is not interpretation; 'Antigone's principle narrows into a private accounting at line 905' is.
4 to 5Handle what does not fit. Top-band candidates address the evidence that complicates their own reading, and are the more persuasive for it.

Self-audit

True of my most recent piece of writing on Antigone
Criterion B1 · Analysis and evaluation of textual features and broader authorial choices · 5 marks

What it rewards

  • Attention to construction: structure, form, narration, staging, sequence, register
  • Evaluation, meaning a judgement about how well a choice works and what it costs
  • Craft vocabulary used accurately and only where it earns its place
  • Broader authorial choices as well as local features: where a scene is placed, what is withheld, who is given the last word

What moves the mark

FromWhat has to change
1 to 2Name a technique and locate it. Nothing counts until the examiner can find it.
2 to 3Explain the effect, not the definition. Never write what a metaphor is.
3 to 4Move up from the local feature to the structural choice: why here, why this order, why withheld.
4 to 5Evaluate. Say what the choice achieves and what it forfeits. 'Sophocles reports the deaths rather than staging them, which sacrifices spectacle to buy the pause in which Kreon's reaction becomes the event.'

Criterion B2 · Comparative analysis · 5 marks

Locked until the partner work is taught. This criterion asks whether the comparison itself does intellectual work, and it cannot be practised on one text.

Criterion C · Focus and organization · 5 marks

What it rewards

  • A thesis that answers the actual question asked
  • Paragraphs organised by idea, never by work
  • Topic sentences that are comparative claims
  • Balance: neither work substantially neglected
  • A development, so that paragraph four could not be swapped with paragraph one

What moves the mark

FromWhat has to change
1 to 2Answer the question in the first paragraph, in one sentence.
2 to 3Give every paragraph a topic sentence that makes a claim rather than announcing a topic.
3 to 4Balance the works and sequence the ideas so the argument escalates.
4 to 5Build in a turn: a paragraph that complicates or qualifies the thesis and then resolves it. Examiners read hundreds of flat essays; a controlled turn is memorable.

Criterion D · Language · 5 marks

What it rewards

  • Precision over decoration
  • Varied sentence length, used deliberately
  • Accurate literary vocabulary
  • Formal register without stiffness, and no filler
  • Control maintained to the last paragraph, which is where it usually fails

What moves the mark

FromWhat has to change
1 to 2Fix the mechanics: tense consistency, agreement, spelling of authors' names and titles.
2 to 3Cut the filler openings. 'In this essay I will', 'Since the beginning of time', 'It is clear that'.
3 to 4Use exact verbs for authorial effect. Not 'shows' every time but juxtaposes, withholds, undercuts, stages, delays, refuses.
4 to 5Vary rhythm on purpose. A short sentence after three long ones is an argumentative move, not a stylistic accident.

10

Exam protocol: 105 minutes

Rehearse this until it is automatic. Candidates lose marks to time management more often than to ignorance. You can practise the first eighteen minutes, choosing and planning, on Antigone alone.

0 to 8 minutes  ·  Choose and unpack
  • Read all four questions twice. Do not choose on the first reading.
  • For each of your two prepared pairings, ask which question it answers best. Choose the question, not the pairing you prefer.
  • Underline the question's key terms and write the alternative meanings of the central term in the margin.
  • Write your thesis as one sentence before you plan anything else.
8 to 18 minutes  ·  Plan
  • Four body paragraphs. Each gets a comparative claim, one reference from each work, and the difference that matters.
  • Order them so the argument escalates. Put your strongest structural or formal point third.
  • Check the balance: mark A and B against each paragraph.
  • Decide your turn: which paragraph complicates the thesis.
18 to 28 minutes  ·  Introduction
  • One sentence framing the shared concern, in the question's terms.
  • Name both works, authors, forms and dates. One clause, no biography.
  • Thesis: a claim with a difference built into it.
  • One sentence signalling the route. No 'firstly, secondly'.
28 to 88 minutes  ·  Four body paragraphs, 15 minutes each
  • Use the six-step paragraph machine. Do not improvise structure under pressure.
  • Both works in every paragraph, always.
  • Re-read the question at the end of each paragraph. Every time.
  • If you are running behind, cut paragraph four to two-thirds rather than losing the conclusion.
88 to 98 minutes  ·  Conclusion
  • State what the comparison produced that neither work alone would have.
  • One sentence on the significance of the difference.
  • Do not introduce new evidence. Do not restate the introduction.
98 to 105 minutes  ·  Proofread
  • Read for error only, not for content. Tense consistency, agreement, spelling of names and titles.
  • Check that every paragraph names both works.
  • Check that the final paragraph is as controlled as the first.

If you are 20 minutes from the end and two paragraphs behind: write the conclusion first, then fill in whatever body paragraph you can. A missing conclusion costs Criterion C directly; a shorter fourth paragraph costs almost nothing.
