01 · Biographical & Historical Context

The World Behind Each Play

Both plays emerge from moments of acute social transition. Ibsen's Norway was grappling with the legal and moral constraints placed on women within bourgeois marriage; Shaw's Edwardian England was navigating the collapse of rigid class boundaries, suffragette activism, and the rise of empirical social science. Understanding these contexts is essential for Paper 2: examiners reward essays that show how literary choices are shaped by and respond to historical forces.

Pygmalion · Shaw (1913)

  • First performed in Vienna (1913); London premiere 1914 with Mrs Patrick Campbell as Eliza
  • Edwardian England: rigid class stratification disguised by shifting economic conditions
  • Women's suffrage movement at its height; the suffragettes were regularly imprisoned
  • Henry Sweet (Oxford phonetician) was the partial model for Higgins; phonetics was a social science promising class-transcendence
  • Shaw had witnessed Ibsen's plays performed in London (1889) and wrote The Quintessence of Ibsenism (1891). Pygmalion is a Shavian answer to the Ibsen "problem play"
  • Shaw's Irish Protestant background gave him the perspective of an outsider on English class: Eliza mirrors Shaw's own linguistic displacement
  • The Pygmalion myth (Ovid, Metamorphoses): the sculptor who falls in love with his own creation, which Shaw deliberately subverts by refusing the romantic ending
  • Shaw attached a lengthy Preface and Epilogue insisting Eliza marries Freddy Eynsford-Hill, not Higgins, and so rejecting audience expectation

A Doll's House · Ibsen (1879)

  • Premiered in Copenhagen, December 1879; caused immediate scandal across Europe
  • Norway: married women had no legal right to borrow money, sign contracts, or own property without a husband's consent (changed 1888)
  • Ibsen denied writing feminist propaganda ("I am not a women's rights advocate; I am a poet"), but the play was received as a feminist manifesto
  • Camilla Collett (Norwegian feminist novelist) and Ibsen's wife Suzannah influenced his understanding of female subjectivity
  • The "alternative ending" (Nora stays, for a German production 1880) was written against Ibsen's will; he called it "a barbarous outrage"
  • Eleanor Marx performed Nora in the famous 1886 Bloomsbury reading, with George Bernard Shaw as Krogstad, a theatrical link between the two playwrights
  • The play belongs to Ibsen's "social realist" cycle (alongside Ghosts, An Enemy of the People), all of which deploy retrospective revelation
  • The three acts follow a single Aristotelian domestic space; the action spans a few days over Christmas, and the festive setting ironically underscores domestic imprisonment

Shared Historical Thread

Both plays are products of the "New Woman" debate (1880s–1910s): the cultural anxiety around women's education, economic independence, and refusal of domestic roles. Ibsen's Nora enacts this by leaving; Shaw's Eliza enacts it by insisting on economic self-sufficiency and refusing to be Higgins's "creation." Both dramatists use the theatre itself as a laboratory for social experiment.

Chronological Context
1879

A Doll's House premieres (Copenhagen)

Ibsen's "problem play" scandalizes bourgeois Europe; the slamming door becomes one of theatre history's most debated stage directions.

1886

Bloomsbury reading: Eleanor Marx as Nora, Shaw as Krogstad

Shaw's direct experience of performing in ADH shapes his understanding of Ibsen's dramatic method, which he theorizes in The Quintessence of Ibsenism (1891).

1891

Shaw publishes The Quintessence of Ibsenism

Shaw champions Ibsen as the originator of the "discussion play": drama in which characters argue about ideas rather than simply enact plot. Pygmalion is Shaw's own contribution to this tradition.

1913

Pygmalion premieres (Vienna Burgtheater)

The play's London run the following year makes Mrs Patrick Campbell a star and transforms the debate about class mobility, language, and female autonomy.

1938

Pygmalion film adaptation (Oscar for Best Screenplay)

Shaw co-writes the screenplay; the romantic ending imposed by the film studio contradicts his epilogue. Shaw's fury reaffirms his insistence on Eliza's independence.

1956

My Fair Lady (Lerner & Loewe musical)

Romanticizes the Higgins/Eliza relationship and erases Shaw's feminist epilogue, a cultural counter-reading that testifies to the play's interpretive instability.

02 · Character Analysis & Comparison

Parallel Characters Across Both Plays

One of Paper 2's most powerful comparative moves is identifying structural parallels between characters in different works. The pairings below are deliberate: both plays operate within the genre of the "problem play" and use similar dramatic architectures to explore gender, class, and power.

PygmalionEliza Doolittle ↔ ADHNora Helmer
The Awakening Woman · Central Protagonist

Both begin the play as figures who have mastered a kind of social performance (Eliza's street-cry, Nora's "skylark" domesticity), and both undergo transformation that ends in a claim for selfhood. Eliza is externally transformed (speech, dress, manner) but achieves internal independence, refusing to be Higgins's "creation." Nora undergoes an internal transformation, stripped of illusion, and ultimately refuses the domestic role she has performed for eight years. Crucially, both assert their right to educate themselves: "I must try to educate myself — you can't help me there; I must do it alone" (Nora); "I shall always be a flower girl to Professor Higgins... but I know I can be a lady to you" (Eliza to Pickering).

Key contrast: Eliza moves from poverty into bourgeois language; Nora moves from bourgeois comfort into existential self-knowledge. Both journeys resist the romantic resolution the genre seems to promise.
PygmalionHenry Higgins ↔ ADHTorvald Helmer
The Male Authority · Architect of the Female Character

Higgins and Torvald are both men who define women through their own desires: Higgins as a scientific experiment ("I have created this thing out of the squashed cabbage leaves of Covent Garden"), Torvald as a decorative doll ("my little squirrel," "my little lark"). Both are revealed, by the play's end, to have fundamentally misunderstood the women they thought they controlled. Higgins is at least honest about his self-absorption: he treats everyone with the same indifference. Torvald is more dangerous because his condescension is wrapped in the language of love and protection.

Key contrast: Higgins is comically self-aware of his misanthropy; Torvald is tragically unself-aware of his paternalism. Shaw uses comedy; Ibsen uses pathos.
PygmalionColonel Pickering ↔ ADHDr. Rank
The Decent Male · Alternative Model of Masculinity

Pickering consistently treats Eliza with respect, and Eliza explicitly credits him: "It was from you that I learnt really nice manners... you always treated me as a lady." Dr. Rank offers Nora genuine friendship and confesses his love, but he is dying: his decency literally cannot survive the world of the play. Both characters represent a form of humane masculinity that the dominant male figure (Higgins/Torvald) cannot embody. Their function is partly structural: they show that the female protagonist's problem is a specific model of patriarchal authority.

Key contrast: Pickering is a living corrective to Higgins; Rank's death removes the only ethical male presence from Nora's life, precipitating her departure.
PygmalionAlfred Doolittle ↔ ADHNils Krogstad
The Social Outsider · Comic / Moral Complication

Alfred Doolittle, Eliza's father and a dustman, provides Shaw's most explicit Marxist critique: "I'm one of the undeserving poor... up agen middle class morality all the time." His transformation into a "gentleman" (via Higgins's ill-considered recommendation) is comic but pointed: bourgeois respectability is a trap. Krogstad is the play's apparent villain but is revealed as a man destroyed by a society that never allowed him redemption from a youthful moral lapse. Both characters expose the hypocrisy of bourgeois moral respectability: society condemns and creates its own outcasts.

Key contrast: Doolittle refuses middle-class morality (comedic); Krogstad is redeemed by Kristine's love (melodramatic resolution Shaw would have satirized).
PygmalionMrs. Higgins ↔ ADHKristine Linde
The Lucid Woman · Voice of Experienced Female Realism

Mrs. Higgins sees through her son's experiment from the start: "You certainly are a pretty pair of babies playing with your live doll." She is the only character who correctly predicts that Eliza will need to be provided for after the experiment ends. Kristine Linde has achieved something Nora has not: genuine economic self-reliance, through sacrifice. Her return to the play serves as a structural mirror: she has lived Nora's potential future, working and surviving alone.

Key contrast: Mrs. Higgins is an observer-corrective; Kristine is a parallel-life mirror. Both illuminate the protagonist's situation from a position of earned wisdom.
PygmalionFreddy Eynsford-Hill ↔ ADHTorvald (re-imagined)
The Romantic Option · The "Weak" Man

Freddy is Shaw's deliberate anti-romantic hero: financially incompetent, emotionally devoted to Eliza, content to be guided by her. Shaw's epilogue insists Eliza marries Freddy because he will not dominate her. The romantic audience finds this unsatisfying; Shaw finds it honest. This parallels the question ADH raises: what would a marriage of equals look like? Ibsen leaves the question open (Nora leaves); Shaw answers it with Freddy, and the answer is deliberately anti-climactic.

Key comparative point: Both plays refuse the "strong man" romantic resolution. Ibsen's refusal is tragic; Shaw's is satiric.
03 · Comparative Themes

Ten Themes for Comparative Analysis

Organising your Paper 2 essay around a shared theme, rather than moving through one play and then the other, is the mark of a top-band response. Each theme below offers a comparative angle with textual anchors for both plays.

1 · Female Identity & Self-Authorship

Both plays dramatise women who must construct a self that social structures have denied them. The question in each case is: who has the right to define a woman's identity?

Shaw: Eliza's identity is literally manufactured by Higgins's linguistic project, but she insists, "I won't be passed over as if I were no account." The epilogue confirms she becomes a shopkeeper: an economic self. Ibsen: Nora's final speech ("I must find out for myself") is the canonical statement of female self-authorship in Western drama. She rejects the identities imposed by father and husband alike.

2 · Language as Power & Class

Language is a site of social control in both plays, though each dramatist explores this differently: Shaw through phonetics as overt class marker; Ibsen through the pet names and registers that infantilise Nora.

Shaw: Cockney dialect marks Eliza as "undeserving poor"; acquiring Received Pronunciation grants social access but also alienates her from her origin ("I have forgotten my own people"). Higgins weaponises linguistic expertise. Ibsen: Torvald's pet names ("skylark," "squirrel," "little featherhead") function as a grammar of control: they reduce Nora to the diminutive. When she refuses these names in Act 3, she enacts linguistic liberation.

3 · Social Performance & the Mask

Both plays explore the theatricality of social identity, the idea that gender and class are performed rather than innate.

Shaw: The entire premise of Pygmalion is that a "lady" is made through performance. The Ascot scene and the Ambassador's party are theatrical tests; the audience watches a performance within a performance. Ibsen: The tarantella sequence is the play's most explicit theatrical metaphor: Nora dances "as if her life depended on it," performing abandon while internally disintegrating. The whole marriage is a theatrical façade: "We have been acting." Johnston (1991) argues the three acts themselves enact three theatrical registers: melodrama, recognition, metaphysical revelation.

4 · Education & Transformation

What does it mean to educate a woman? Both plays interrogate the politics of female education: who controls it, what it produces, and who benefits.

Shaw: Higgins's "education" of Eliza is an act of appropriation disguised as benevolence; he takes the credit. Eliza rejects this: "I know that I shall always be a flower girl to Professor Higgins because he always treats me as a flower girl." Pickering's respectful treatment is the true education. Ibsen: Nora resolves to educate herself: "I must try to educate myself — you can't help me there." Her self-education is moral and philosophical, a discovery of her own ignorance of "the world and of herself."

5 · Marriage, Domesticity & the Doll's House

Both plays examine marriage as an institution that deforms the women within it through economic dependency, infantilisation, and the performance of happiness.

Shaw: Shaw refuses to end his play in marriage (despite audience pressure for a Higgins-Eliza union), insisting in the Epilogue that such a marriage would recreate the power imbalance the play critiques. Eliza's marriage to Freddy is Shaw's version of an egalitarian alternative. Ibsen: "Our home has been nothing but a playroom. I have been your doll-wife, just as at home I was Papa's doll-child." Nora's metaphor of the "doll's house" gives the play its title and its central image of bourgeois marriage as a beautiful cage.

6 · Class Mobility & Social Hypocrisy

Both plays expose the gap between bourgeois ideology (merit, virtue, respectability) and bourgeois practice (money, inheritance, social reputation).

Shaw: Alfred Doolittle's comic transformation into "a prisoner of middle class morality" after Higgins's legacy satirises the idea that class is a moral rather than economic category. Eliza's transformation reveals that "ladyhood" is simply performance backed by money. Ibsen: Torvald's obsession with reputation ("No man can be expected to sacrifice his honour for his wife") reveals bourgeois morality as self-interest masquerading as principle. Krogstad is destroyed for threatening Torvald's social image.

7 · Money, Debt & Economic Subjugation

Economic structures enable and constrain both protagonists; money drives each plot.

Shaw: Eliza's situation is entirely economic from the start: she pays Higgins five shillings, is funded by Pickering, and the experiment's success leaves her economically stranded ("What's to become of me?"). Her independence is achieved when she sets up a flower shop. Ibsen: Nora's secret loan, forged to save Torvald's life, is the dramatic engine of the play. Ibsen shows how women's legal exclusion from financial life forced them into deceit. The money plot strips away the romantic surface of the Helmer marriage.

8 · The Myth of the Creator & the Created

Both plays engage explicitly with the myth of Pygmalion: the (male) artist who creates a (female) object and mistakes creation for relationship.

Shaw: Higgins openly claims his Pygmalion role: "I have created this thing." Shaw's Epilogue directly references the myth, noting that "Galatea never does quite like Pygmalion: his relation to her is too godlike to be altogether agreeable." The play is Shaw's deliberate deconstruction of the male-creator fantasy. Ibsen: Torvald has been Nora's Pygmalion too, shaping her into his ideal doll-wife. Nora's recognition is the Galatea who realises she was always alive: "I believe that before all else I am a reasonable human being."

9 · The Problem of the Ending

Both plays are famous for their controversial, anti-romantic endings, which refuse resolution and demand that the audience engage with an uncomfortable question.

Shaw: The stage ending leaves ambiguous whether Eliza will return; Shaw's Epilogue insists she does not. Audiences and directors routinely defy Shaw, staging a romantic reunion; this reception history illustrates how powerfully the play's own social critique is resisted. Ibsen: The slamming door is one of theatre's most famous exits. The German alternative ending (Nora stays) was demanded by a producer who found Nora's departure immoral, which reveals how radical Ibsen's original choice was. The ending refuses to resolve the "woman question" it raises.

10 · Genre Conventions & their Subversion

Both playwrights use and simultaneously undermine theatrical genre conventions (the "well-made play," romantic comedy, melodrama) to produce their critique.

Shaw: Pygmalion's five-act structure resembles a romantic comedy (mismatched pair + transformation + resolution), but Shaw refuses the expected marriage. The preface and epilogue, unusual formal additions, allow Shaw to argue with his own play's reception. Ibsen: Johnston (1991) argues ADH deploys three theatrical registers across its three acts: Act 1 as melodrama (villain threatens heroine), Act 2 as theatrical self-consciousness, Act 3 as metaphysical discussion, deliberately breaking the "well-made play" form from within.

04 · Form, Structure & Genre

How Each Play is Built

Paper 2 Criterion B specifically rewards analysis of how authors' choices of form, structure, and language shape meaning. The structural differences between Pygmalion and A Doll's House enact different philosophies of drama.

Pygmalion · Shaw
  • Five acts, expansive and episodic; they cover months of transformation (Act 1: street; Act 2: Wimpole St.; Acts 3–4: social tests; Act 5: confrontation)
  • Prose comedy with Shavian wit: rapid repartee, rhetorical speeches, comic reversals
  • Preface + Epilogue, a paratextual apparatus of unusual extent; Shaw uses these to control interpretation and argue with his audience
  • Stage directions as essays. Shaw's elaborate stage directions describe character psychology, social context, and authorial intention in prose equivalent to a novel
  • Open structure: time passes between acts; the play moves forward (prospective: what will Eliza become?)
  • Genre subversion: adopts romantic comedy arc but refuses romantic resolution; uses mythic frame (Pygmalion/Galatea) ironically
  • The "discussion play" (Shaw's term, derived from Ibsen): Act 5 is largely dialectical debate between Eliza and Higgins about the nature of their relationship
  • Multiple public/private spaces (the street, drawing room, garden party, Embassy), each registering a different social register and test of Eliza's performance
A Doll's House · Ibsen
  • Three acts, tight and compressed; the entire action takes place over three days at Christmas (unity of time approximated)
  • Single domestic setting: the Helmer living room, whose confinement of space enacts Nora's confinement of life
  • Unity of action. One central crisis (the bond/letter) drives all three acts, and no subplots distract from the main confrontation
  • Retrospective revelation: the audience progressively discovers what has already happened (Nora's loan, the forgery) rather than watching events unfold; this is Ibsen's signature structural innovation
  • Johnston's "Three Stages" (1991): Act 1 = melodrama (Krogstad as villain, Nora as threatened heroine); Act 2 = theatrical self-consciousness (the tarantella, Nora as performer); Act 3 = metaphysical discussion (the departure)
  • The "well-made play" (pièce bien faite). Ibsen inherits Scribe's tight plotting but uses it to expose bourgeois illusion rather than confirm it
  • Prose naturalism: dialogue mimics natural speech; characters talk across each other, leave sentences unfinished, change subject under emotional pressure
  • Symbolic objects all carry structural weight: the Christmas tree (domestic ideal decaying), the letter box (the intrusion of the outside world), the tarantella costume

Key Structural Comparative Point

Where Ibsen's structure works backwards (excavating the past to reveal the foundation of the present), Shaw's structure works forwards (following a transformation through time). This difference enacts their respective philosophies: Ibsen believes truth is buried beneath social surfaces; Shaw believes truth is constructed through social argument.

05 · Language, Style & Dramatic Devices

The Craft of Each Playwright

In Paper 2, "analysis" means showing how specific language choices create meaning, rather than reporting what happens. The following devices are particularly productive for comparative analysis, as both playwrights employ them but to distinctly different effects.

Dialect & Register ▸ expand

Shaw uses phonetic spelling (Cockney dialect) in Eliza's early speech as a visual and sonic marker of class difference, a theatrical device that makes the audience hear inequality. As Eliza acquires RP, her language transforms but so does her access to social spaces. Language is literally the play's subject matter.

"I'm a good girl, I am." / "The rain in Spain stays mainly in the plain." The pivot runs from self-protective assertion to phonetic exercise to, finally, authentic self-expression.

Ibsen uses register shifts more subtly. Torvald's pet-name register ("my little skylark," "little featherhead") is contrasted with Nora's own register in Act 3, when she speaks in full sentences, without diminutives: "I have been greatly wronged, Torvald." The shift in register is the linguistic enactment of her awakening.

"Now you must go": Nora's dismissal of Torvald in Act 3. The syntax is reversed; it is she who now directs him.

Dramatic Irony ▸ expand

Shaw uses dramatic irony throughout the social performance scenes: the audience, unlike the party guests, knows Eliza is a Cockney flower girl. This creates a satirical comedy: what does it say about class if Eliza can "pass" simply by speaking differently? The irony is directed at the arbitrariness of class distinction.

At Ascot, Eliza's grammatically perfect but semantically inappropriate remarks about a relative's death ("She had a drop, and it was her downfall") are comic precisely because the audience understands the gap between form and content.

Ibsen deploys retrospective dramatic irony; we know, before Torvald does, the nature of the letter. The audience watches Torvald perform his heroic self-image ("If the crisis comes, you will find I am man enough to take everything upon myself") knowing the letter will expose this as hollow posturing.

Nora's preparations for suicide while playing with her children; the audience understands what Torvald cannot see.

Symbolic Objects & Stage Properties ▸ expand

Both playwrights invest physical objects with symbolic weight, converting the material surface of realism into metaphor.

Pygmalion: Eliza's flower basket (her original identity and livelihood); her dress transformation (the body as class text); Higgins's phonograph and recording equipment (the reduction of a human voice to data). The final image of the play, Eliza's absence, is itself a kind of symbolic object: the shape of what she has refused to be.

A Doll's House: The Christmas tree (domestic ideal; it is stripped and decaying by Act 3, Ibsen's most explicit symbolic stage direction); the letter box (the intrusion of the external, legal world into the sealed domestic sphere); Nora's tarantella costume (the performance of femininity literally worn on her body); the bond/promissory note (the material document of women's legal non-existence).

The "Discussion" / Argument Scene ▸ expand

Both plays culminate in a long argumentative confrontation between the central characters, a "discussion" in Shaw's technical sense, where the audience witnesses a debate about ideas rather than a plot resolution.

Pygmalion Act 5: Eliza and Higgins's extended argument about what she owes him; she refuses his terms; he refuses to acknowledge her independence. The scene ends without resolution: Higgins calls out his shopping list, and Eliza may or may not comply. Shaw refuses to resolve the debate theatrically.

ADH Act 3: Nora and Torvald's "serious conversation" is the longest discussion scene in Ibsen. Nora methodically dismantles Torvald's self-understanding and her own. Shaw was directly influenced by this scene when theorizing the "discussion play" in his 1891 essay on Ibsen.

Tone & Comic vs. Tragic Register ▸ expand

This is a crucial formal difference. Shaw deploys wit and satire to deliver social critique; Ibsen uses pathos and emotional realism. Both modes are politically purposeful.

Shaw: Higgins's self-congratulatory speeches are funny, and the comedy is the critique. The audience laughs at Higgins's obliviousness, which makes the laughter complicit in the system he represents. Shaw's comedy makes the audience uncomfortable about its own amusement.

Ibsen: The emotional realism of Nora's final exit makes the audience feel the weight of what she is doing. Unlike Shaw's satire, Ibsen's pathos refuses the audience a comfortable distance; the "slamming door" is designed to reverberate in the auditorium and in the audience's conscience.

Comparative angle: Both playwrights use their chosen register, comedy and pathos respectively, to deny the audience comfortable resolution.

Stage Directions & Authorial Voice ▸ expand

Both playwrights use stage directions as a literary genre in their own right, but in strikingly different ways.

Shaw: His stage directions are quasi-novelistic essays, full of irony, social commentary, and direct address to the reader/director. The introduction of Higgins ("a professor of phonetics... He is of the energetic, scientific type, heartily, even violently interested in everything that can be studied as a scientific subject...") is a character analysis. Shaw uses stage directions to control interpretation.

Ibsen: Stage directions are spare and precise; they indicate exactly what needs to be visible. "She takes off her hat and coat." The minimalism forces the actor and director to fill the silences. The famous final direction "A door is heard shutting" is all that marks the most famous exit in theatre history.

06 · Critical Lenses

Reading Both Plays Through Theory

A top Paper 2 response demonstrates an understanding of how different critical frameworks illuminate different aspects of the texts. Use these lenses to generate comparative arguments of your own.

Feminist Criticism
Female Awakening & Patriarchal Constraint

Both plays are foundational feminist texts, though both playwrights complicated this reading. Ibsen denied writing feminist propaganda; Shaw's feminism is mediated through satire rather than earnest advocacy. Gail Finney (in the Cambridge Companion to Ibsen) documents the enthusiastic feminist reception of ADH across Europe: it was performed in radical and suffragette circles, with Eleanor Marx as the first notable English Nora. J. Ellen Gainor argues in Shaw's Daughters (1991) that Shaw constructed female characters as intellectual equals whose ultimate freedom he championed, while through the Higgins figure he replicated the male-mentor pattern he was critiquing. The plays thus offer a feminist reading AND a critique of the limits of male-authored feminism.

How does each playwright use female characters to challenge the ideology of "separate spheres"?
To what extent can a play written by a man enact a genuinely feminist vision?
Compare the moments of female "awakening" in each play: what triggers them, and what do they cost?
Marxist / Materialist Criticism
Class, Money & Bourgeois Ideology

Both plays expose how bourgeois ideology (respectability, moral virtue, the sanctity of marriage) serves economic interests. In Pygmalion, Alfred Doolittle's complaint against "middle class morality" is Shaw at his most explicitly Marxist: virtue is the luxury of those who do not need money. Eliza's transformation shows that "class" is produced by access to capital and education. In ADH, Nora's crime, forging a bond to save her husband's life, reveals how women's legal exclusion from financial life forced dishonesty upon them. Torvald's horror at Krogstad's letter is economic fear: "My career, my reputation."

How does each play show that "morality" is a function of economic position?
Compare the role of money and debt in driving each plot: what does this reveal about the characters' "freedom"?
How does Shaw's satire of class differ from Ibsen's critique of bourgeois domestic ideology?
Psychoanalytic Criticism
Narcissism, Repression & the Unconscious

Carol Strongin Tufts ("Recasting A Doll House: Narcissism as Character Motivation," Comparative Drama, 1986) argues that Nora's behaviour displays DSM criteria for Narcissistic Personality Disorder: grandiosity (her secret heroism narrative), exhibitionism (the tarantella), lack of empathy (her use of Kristine and Rank), and entitlement. This reading complicates the purely sympathetic feminist Nora and enriches her as a psychologically complex figure shaped by a society that channelled women's intelligence into manipulation. Similarly, Higgins in Pygmalion can be read as a narcissist who cannot distinguish between Eliza as a person and Eliza as his project; his inability to understand her anger is the mark of classic narcissistic non-empathy.

How does the concept of narcissism (Tufts) complicate a straightforwardly feminist reading of Nora?
Compare Higgins and Torvald as variants of male narcissism: what differs in how each playwright renders this psychologically?
What does the tarantella reveal about the relationship between repression and performance?
Postcolonial Criticism
Language, Otherness & the Colonised Voice

Declan Kiberd (Inventing Ireland, 1995) reads Shaw through the lens of Irish colonial experience: as an Irishman writing in England, Shaw understood linguistic "otherness" from the inside. Eliza Doolittle, whose Cockney accent marks her as socially "other," mirrors the position of the Irish or colonial subject whose native speech is treated as inferior. Higgins, the English phonetician who "corrects" Eliza's speech, becomes a figure of linguistic colonialism. This lens opens a productive comparative question: in ADH, Norwegian provincial society similarly imposes on Nora a discourse of femininity that functions as a colonial discourse, defining the "native" (woman) by the norms of the coloniser (bourgeois patriarchy).

How does Kiberd's postcolonial reading of Shaw enrich or complicate Eliza's story?
In what ways does each play stage the experience of having one's "native" identity overwritten by a dominant discourse?
Compare the ending of each play as an act of "decolonisation": is this a useful metaphor?
New Historicism
Plays as Historical Documents

New historicism insists that literary texts are embedded in history: they are produced by, and in turn produce, the cultural conditions of their moment. Pygmalion (1913) is a document of the pre-First World War moment when class was still rigid enough to be satirised but also permeable enough to be "performed"; the social anxiety about upward mobility is legible in the play's central experiment. ADH (1879) is a document of the specifically Norwegian legal moment (married women's property rights were achieved in Norway in 1888) and of the pan-European feminist movement. Reading both plays historicistically recovers the radical provocation they represented when first performed.

What specific historical conditions does each play respond to, and how are these encoded in its form?
How does knowing the historical context of each play's first performance change your understanding of its "radical" quality?
Compare the different ways in which each play was censored, altered, or resisted in its reception history.
07 · Key Scholars & Critics

Critical Voices to Deploy in Paper 2

Integrating scholarly perspectives is expected at HL. Deploy these critics to support, nuance, or argue against your own interpretations, never simply to summarise. The most impressive essays use critics to generate tension.

Brian Johnston
"Three Stages of A Doll House," Comparative Drama 25.4 (Winter 1991–92), pp. 311–328

Johnston argues that the three acts of ADH represent three progressive theatrical stages, situating Ibsen within a post-Kantian German dramatic-philosophic tradition (from Lessing to Brecht). Act 1 operates as melodrama: conventional theatrical pleasure, the sympathetic heroine threatened by the unscrupulous villain (Krogstad). Act 2 introduces self-consciousness about theatrical illusion; the tarantella is Nora performing performance. Act 3 achieves what Johnston calls metaphysical self-knowledge, in which the "discussion" breaks the conventions of realist drama and stages a philosophical confrontation. Johnston draws on Benjamin Bennett's Modern Drama and German Classicism to argue that Ibsen's theatrical devices are meant to be recognised as devices, creating a "theater of truth" beyond social realism. This is a powerful framework for discussing ADH's form and for comparative analysis with Pygmalion, which Shaw himself described in terms of three theatrical traditions: comedy, romance, and "discussion."

Carol Strongin Tufts
"Recasting A Doll House: Narcissism as Character Motivation in Ibsen's Play," Comparative Drama 20.2 (Summer 1986), pp. 140–159

Tufts opens by citing Ibsen's own disavowal of feminist propaganda and argues that a contemporary production of ADH might productively reframe the play through the clinical lens of narcissistic personality disorder (using DSM criteria: grandiosity, preoccupation with fantasies of unlimited success, exhibitionism, lack of empathy, sense of entitlement, exploitativeness). She applies this to Nora's relationships with Kristine Linde (whom she uses instrumentally), Dr. Rank (whom she manipulates with exhibitionistic flirtation), and Torvald (toward whom she displays entitlement and lack of empathy). This reading is valuable for Paper 2 because it opens an alternative to the purely sympathetic feminist Nora and produces a more psychologically complex character, one who has been shaped by a society that channelled women's intelligence into manipulation. It also complicates the Higgins/Nora comparison: both may be narcissists, but they are produced by different social conditions.

Gail Finney
"Ibsen and Feminism," in The Cambridge Companion to Ibsen, ed. James McFarlane (Cambridge UP)

Finney documents the enthusiastic feminist reception of ADH across Europe, noting that the 1886 Bloomsbury reading (Eleanor Marx as Nora, George Bernard Shaw as Krogstad) directly linked the two playwrights in the feminist tradition. She covers Ibsen's own complex relationship to feminism (he denied writing propaganda, but supported married women's property rights and was influenced by Camilla Collett), and traces the alternative-ending controversy (Nora stays for the German production, Ibsen's "barbarous outrage"). The essay establishes ADH as culturally foundational for what Finney calls the feminist drama of awakening, while resisting a simple equation of Ibsen with feminist doctrine. A useful scholarly counterweight to purely biographical or theatrical readings.

J. Ellen Gainor
Shaw's Daughters: Dramatic and Narrative Constructions of Gender (University of Michigan Press, 1991)

Gainor's feminist study is the essential scholarly text on gender in Shaw. She argues that Shaw constructed female characters, including Eliza, as "daughters": figures he educated, shaped, and intellectually championed, but who remained trapped within a male-created narrative. The Higgins-Eliza relationship replicates, within the play, the very paternalism the play appears to critique. Gainor uses psychoanalytic and gender theory alongside close dramatic analysis. Her reading of Pygmalion's ending is particularly relevant: she argues that Eliza's independence is genuine, but that Shaw's own narrative authority (the Epilogue) continues to control and define her: the author as Pygmalion of his own creation. This is a direct parallel to Torvald's relationship with Nora.

Raymond Williams
Drama from Ibsen to Brecht (Oxford University Press, 1968)

Williams's comparative study is uniquely valuable for Paper 2 because it discusses both Ibsen and Shaw within the same critical framework. Williams argues that Shaw's drama transforms the Ibsenite "problem play" through comedy and wit but shares Ibsen's fundamental critique of bourgeois society. Where Ibsen uses emotional realism and pathos to expose social contradiction, Shaw uses intellectual comedy; both, Williams argues, refuse to offer resolution. Williams's concept of the drama of "social naturalism" (plays that use realistic surfaces to expose the structures beneath) applies to both ADH and Pygmalion. His analysis of how Shaw adapted Ibsen's "discussion" technique is directly relevant to comparing the Act 3 scenes of each play.

Declan Kiberd
Inventing Ireland: The Literature of the Modern Nation (Harvard University Press, 1995)

Kiberd's postcolonial reading of Shaw argues that as an Irishman writing in and about England, Shaw understood linguistic "otherness" from the inside. Eliza Doolittle, whose Cockney accent marks her as socially inferior, mirrors the colonial subject whose native speech is deemed defective by the colonising culture. Higgins, the English phonetician, becomes a figure of linguistic authority whose "correction" of Eliza's speech is an act of cultural colonisation. Kiberd's framework opens the postcolonial angle in Pygmalion and provides a rich comparative lens alongside the class and gender readings. The chapter on Shaw runs to approximately 40 pages and is one of the most sophisticated available analyses of Shaw's Irish identity and its impact on his drama.

Margery Morgan
The Shavian Playground: An Exploration of the Art of George Bernard Shaw (Methuen, 1972)

Morgan's study of Shaw's dramatic art contains a substantial chapter on Pygmalion that analyses the play across three dimensions: the mythic (the Pygmalion/Galatea story and its deliberate subversion), the social (class and phonetics as a critique of Edwardian society), and the linguistic (Shaw's use of dialect and register as dramatic and comic devices). Morgan is attentive to Shaw's ambivalence about his own heroine: his epilogue simultaneously liberates and controls Eliza. Her analysis of how Shaw's comic tone both enables and limits his social critique is directly applicable to comparison with Ibsen's more straightforwardly earnest approach.

Michael Holroyd
Bernard Shaw, Vol. 2: The Pursuit of Power, 1898–1918 (Chatto & Windus, 1989)

Holroyd's definitive four-volume biography covers Pygmalion's genesis in considerable depth, including Shaw's relationship with Mrs Patrick Campbell (who created Eliza), his deliberate resistance to the romantic ending, and his fury at the film adaptation's imposed resolution. Holroyd situates Pygmalion within Shaw's extended engagement with Ibsen and the "New Woman" debate. Biographical context from Holroyd should be used carefully in Paper 2, to illuminate specific authorial choices (such as the Epilogue's insistence on Eliza's independence). Holroyd also discusses how Shaw's Irish background shaped his outsider perspective on English class, which is useful alongside Kiberd.

Joan Templeton
Ibsen's Women (Cambridge University Press, 1997)

Templeton's study argues against critics who have minimised the feminist dimension of Ibsen's work. She reads Nora as a paradigm of female awakening that transcends its historical moment, a character whose "discovery" of her own selfhood is the central drama of bourgeois modernity. Templeton directly engages with critics (including those who see Nora as narcissistic or psychologically unstable) and argues that these readings are symptomatic of the very patriarchal culture the play critiques: the woman who insists on selfhood is diagnosed as disordered. This debate between Templeton and Tufts is ideal material for a Paper 2 essay on the "ambiguity" of Nora's awakening.

10 · Paper 2 Exam Preparation

Writing the Comparative Essay

Paper 2 is a 1 hour 45 minute comparative essay (HL). You write on two works studied in the course. You have NO access to the texts. The exam is marked out of 20 across four criteria.

A
Knowledge, Understanding & Interpretation
5 marks

Show deep, accurate knowledge of BOTH works. Interpret them perceptively and go beyond plot summary. Connect textual detail to thematic significance.

B
Analysis & Evaluation
5 marks

Analyse HOW literary choices (form, structure, language) create meaning. Evaluate the effect of specific techniques. Cite briefly but precisely. Analyse the function of every device you identify.

C
Focus & Organisation
5 marks

Sustain a coherent comparative argument. Organise by idea/theme, not by play. Each paragraph should move between both texts. Use comparative connectives purposefully.

D
Language
5 marks

Precise literary vocabulary, formal register, varied syntax. Avoid colloquialisms. Use terms like: dramatic irony, retrospective revelation, naturalism, social critique, patriarchal, bourgeois, register, genre subversion.

Practice with these Paper 2-style questions. For each, identify: the key debate the question sets up, the three themes you would organise around, and one scholar for each work you would deploy.

"Writers use dramatic conventions to both reinforce and challenge social norms." Compare the ways in which Shaw and Ibsen use theatrical form to challenge social expectations in Pygmalion and A Doll's House.

Organise your essay by idea, not by play. Avoid the "tennis-match" structure (three paragraphs on Shaw, then three on Ibsen). Instead, move between both texts within every paragraph, unified by a single comparative argument.

1

Introduction (10 minutes writing)

Open with the central comparative tension. State your thesis: what is the key similarity AND key difference between how the two plays handle the topic the question raises? Name both works, authors, dates, and genres. Establish your critical framework (e.g., feminist, Marxist) if relevant. End with a roadmap sentence indicating your three main comparative points.

2

Body Paragraph 1: Comparative Point A (16 minutes)

Open with a topic sentence that makes a comparative claim covering BOTH plays. Develop your argument with textual evidence from Play 1 → analysis → connection to Play 2 → evidence from Play 2 → analysis → synthesis of the comparison. Include one scholar reference. End with a sentence that advances your overall thesis.

3

Body Paragraph 2: Comparative Point B (16 minutes)

Introduce a new comparative angle, ideally one that complicates or nuances the argument of Paragraph 1. You might introduce an alternative critical reading here (e.g., use Tufts's narcissism reading to complicate the feminist reading of Nora). Maintain the comparative structure: both plays in every paragraph.

4

Body Paragraph 3: Form, Structure, or Language Focus (16 minutes)

Paper 2 Criterion B specifically rewards analysis of literary form. Devote at least one paragraph to comparing HOW each playwright constructs meaning (structure, genre choices, dramatic technique, use of stage directions, symbolic objects) rather than WHAT the plays say. This is where Johnston's "three stages" argument or Shaw's preface/epilogue apparatus are most useful.

5

Conclusion (8 minutes)

Synthesise rather than summarise. Return to the question's exact phrasing. State your overall comparative judgement: in what sense are the two plays similar in their handling of the question's concern, and in what sense does each playwright's distinct formal and tonal choices produce a different relationship to that concern? End with a sentence that gestures toward the plays' ongoing relevance, without being vague or clichéd.

The following is a model comparative paragraph in response to the question: "Writers present female characters as defined by their relationships to male authority, but also as capable of resisting it."

Topic Sentence: Comparative Claim

Both Shaw and Ibsen construct female protagonists who are initially defined entirely by male authority (Eliza through Higgins's linguistic experiment, Nora through Torvald's system of pet names and domestic management), yet both playwrights ensure that the female characters ultimately expose, and reject, the male epistemology that has confined them.

Evidence + Analysis (Shaw → Ibsen → Synthesis)

In Pygmalion, Higgins's claim that "I have created this thing out of the squashed cabbage leaves of Covent Garden" frames Eliza as raw material rather than person: the Pygmalion myth literalised. Shaw's use of the dehumanising demonstrative "this thing" encodes in syntax the violence of the male-creation fantasy. Yet this is precisely the construction Eliza dismantles in Act 5: "The difference between a lady and a flower girl is not how she behaves, but how she's treated". The formulation relocates the origin of identity from the creator to the social relationship, and implicitly prosecutes Higgins for the identity he claims to have conferred. Ibsen enacts a parallel but tonally contrasting movement in A Doll's House: Torvald's register of diminutives ("my little skylark," "little featherhead") grammatically miniaturises Nora throughout the first two acts, reducing her to a domestic ornament. Nora's resistance is registered, as Finney (in the Cambridge Companion to Ibsen) observes, in the progressive withdrawal of performed femininity: the tarantella (in which she dances "as if her life depended on it") is simultaneously the last performance of the doll-wife and the first expression of a self that can no longer contain itself within the role. Where Shaw's critique of male authority is filtered through comedy (Higgins's obliviousness is funny, which makes it bearable), Ibsen's is delivered through the pathos of a woman discovering she has been "greatly wronged" by every man who claimed to love her. Both strategies deny the audience comfortable resolution: Shaw refuses romantic closure; Ibsen stages an exit that cannot be aestheticised.

Synthesis Sentence

The formal difference between Shaw's satiric comedy and Ibsen's emotional realism reinforces the shared ideological argument: that the structures of male authority are systemic formations, legible in the grammar of daily life, and dismantlable only through the female character's willingness to step outside the genre the play appears to inhabit.

Using varied and precise comparative language is a Criterion C and D requirement. Avoid repeating "similarly" and "in contrast." The following connectives and formulations create sophisticated, nuanced comparison.

Similarity & Parallel

Both playwrights... Similarly, Ibsen / Shaw... In both plays, the effect of... This mirrors the moment in which... Shaw, like Ibsen, deploys... A comparable function is served by... The parallel is instructive: both...

Contrast & Difference

Whereas Shaw..., Ibsen... In contrast to Ibsen's earnest naturalism, Shaw... Where Pygmalion deploys satire..., ADH relies on... The distinction, however, is significant... Unlike Nora, Eliza... This divergence enacts... The tonal gulf between the two plays is itself meaningful...

Nuance & Complication

This reading is complicated, however, by... Tufts's reading of Nora as narcissistic complicates... Shaw's own Epilogue partially undermines... The tension between... and... is not resolved but... What unites the two plays is not similarity of conclusion but... Both plays resist, though differently...
