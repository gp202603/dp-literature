Section 01

Context & Background

Biographical Context

The play is strongly autobiographical. Fugard had a Black friend and mentor named Sam Semela who worked in his mother's St. George's Park Tea Room in Port Elizabeth. The kite, the spitting incident, and the "Whites Only" bench are all drawn from Fugard's real life. Writing the play was, for Fugard, an act of moral reckoning.

Historical Context: 1950 South Africa

The play is set in 1950, the year Errol Durbach calls "that annus mirabilis of Apartheid legislation." The National Party won power in 1948 and began legislating racial segregation: the Group Areas Act (1950), the Population Registration Act, the Immorality Act. Apartheid was daily legal violence.

The Setting: St. George's Park Tea Room, Port Elizabeth

The single-room setting is deliberate. A public commercial space (front stage) that is also a domestic refuge for three characters who are trapped together across the race line. The tea room is a microcosm of apartheid society. Hierarchy is encoded in the furniture, in body positions, and in every spoken title.

Section 02

Plot Structure & Arc

Exposition

Sam and Willie dance. Hally arrives from school. His father is in hospital, which gives him a temporary reprieve.

Rising Action

Men of Magnitude discussion. Kite memory. Sam's utopian dance vision. A second phone call confirms the father is coming home.

Crisis / Climax

Racist joke. Hally demands "Master Harold." The spit.

Section 03

Character Analysis

Sam Semela
Waiter · Surrogate Father · Moral Philosopher

Sam is the moral centre of the play. He built a kite to lift Hally's shame after his father's drunken humiliation; he taught Hally geography; he tried to form Hally's moral imagination. His patience and love are a conscious choice, costly in a way Hally never grasps.

Goffman: Sam wears the waiter's coat as a front-stage performance that conceals his intelligence and his moral authority. When he reads comic books, he is backstage. Fanon: Sam chooses non-violence as a survival strategy in a world where resistance means deportation. Hegel: Sam's dignity remains unbroken even when Hally demands submission. He is the true master in the dialectic.

Moral centre Surrogate father Non-violent Front-stage performance Educator Gerasim parallel (Urban)
Hally (Harold)
White schoolboy · Tragic Figure of Complicity

Hally is a child of a broken home, the son of an alcoholic and racist father, who has genuinely loved and been loved by Sam. His tragedy is that under emotional crisis, he reaches for the tools of his oppressor class. Durbach calls his racism a "psychopathology", so internalised that it surfaces automatically, beyond conscious control.

Freud: Hally cannot confront his shame about his father's body, so he displaces that disgust onto Sam (the spit transfers the "filth" of disability onto the Black man). Goffman: the demand for "Master Harold" is an emergency impression management move. Hally reasserts social hierarchy when his sense of self is threatened. Hegel: in demanding submission, Hally achieves the opposite of freedom.

Complicit Displacement (Freud) Apartheid's child Intelligent Genuinely loves Sam Capable of change?
Willie Malopo
Waiter · Mirror & Foil · Figure of Hope

Willie functions as a symbolic parallel and as the play's quiet resolution. His beating of Hilda mirrors the vertical violence Hally enacts on Sam; both reproduce cycles of oppression absorbed from those above them. Yet Willie's arc is hopeful: at the end, he resolves to stop beating Hilda and spends his bus fare so that Sam can dance. The dream is kept alive.

Mirror of oppression Arc of hope Comic relief Final dancer
Section 04

Themes

Racism & Apartheid

Apartheid is present in every object and body position before a word is spoken. The "Whites Only" bench builds racism into the landscape itself. Hally's racism is "psychopathological" (Durbach), so internalised it surfaces involuntarily. The system reproduces itself through the people it victimises (Willie → Hilda).

Education & Wisdom

Whose knowledge counts? Hally has school facts; Sam has lived wisdom. The "Men of Magnitude" discussion is saturated with irony. Hally nominates Tolstoy while embodying everything Tolstoy opposed. Sam has been educating Hally all along, but the system can never acknowledge this.

Dance & Utopia

Dance = "a world in which accidents don't happen", the ideal set against the real. Ballroom dancing historically descends from African and lower-class roots (Hoegberg), so Sam and Willie's hobby is an act of cultural reclamation. The final dance is an act of dignity and political resistance.

Father Figures & Family

Hally has two fathers: his biological father (alcoholic, racist, shaming) and his surrogate father Sam (loving, educational, morally serious). His tragedy is choosing the broken biological model over the genuine one. Hegel's dialectic: Hally makes himself more dependent and less free by insisting on dominance.

Hope & Despair

The play oscillates: moments of genuine connection shattered by apartheid's logic. Sam's final words offer conditional hope, since Hally CAN walk away from the bench. Willie's resolution not to beat Hilda = the smallest possible redemption arc. Fugard himself walked away from it: this is the autobiographical hope embedded in the ending.

Section 05

Symbols & Motifs

The Kite
Sam built Hally a kite when he was depressed
Freedom, transcendence, love without condition. The Whites Only bench means Sam cannot share the joy he created. That tragic gap sits at the centre of their relationship.
The Whites Only Bench
A racial segregation law
The foundational injustice. Hally sits on it without noticing, because privilege is invisible to those who hold it. Sam's challenge: "You can walk away from it any time you choose."
Ballroom Dance
Sam & Willie's hobby and competition
Utopian symbol: mutual respect, cooperation, no collisions. Cultural reclamation: African rhythms lie at the root of European ballroom. The dream of non-racial society made physical.
The Spit
Hally spits in Sam's face
The play's most violent act. Fanon: Hally transfers the "filth" of his father's disability and shame onto the Black man. The ultimate settler act turns the Other into a receptacle for your own abjection.
The Chamberpot
Hally empties his father's chamberpot
Hally is disgusted by the disabled father's body. Contrasted with Gerasim (Tolstoy's Ivan Ilych), who performs this task lovingly. Urban: Hally rejects Gerasim-like selfless service.
Sarah Vaughan Song
Plays as Sam & Willie dance at the end
Grace and dignity in Black artistry, persisting after Hally's cruelty as a benediction. Willie's sacrifice (bus fare) enables the dream to be enacted.
The Telephone
Calls from Hally's mother
The outside world invading the temporary safety of the tea room. The apartheid system reaches in through a father's body and through a phone call, destabilising everything.
Waiter's Coat
Sam's uniform
Goffman: the front-stage costume. When Sam puts it on, he performs the role society has assigned him. But the coat can be removed; it does not define his inner self.
Section 06

Critical Perspectives

The IB expects you to apply theoretical lenses as analytical tools. Use them to generate specific insights from specific textual moments.

A. Erving Goffman: Dramaturgical Theory
The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life, 1959
Sociological

All social life is performance. People manage their image for an audience. Front stage: public space where the performance is on. Back stage: private space where the performer relaxes their role. Impression management: the constant work of maintaining a credible social front.

ConceptApplication to the PlayTextual Evidence
FRONT STAGE The tea room = front stage. Sam and Willie perform deference professionally. "At your service, Master Harold!" is an exaggerated performance of servility.
BACK STAGE Servants' quarters = their genuine private self. Hally is punished for crossing this frontier. Hally sneaks to their quarters as a child and is "given a rowing" for crossing the boundary.
IMPRESSION MANAGEMENT Hally demands "Master Harold" when his performance of "enlightened white boy" collapses. Emergency reassertion of his front-stage social role to regain control.
PERFORMANCE BREAKDOWN The spit = total collapse of Hally's performance of being above racism. Sam: "You don't know what you've just done." Even Sam's composed front begins to crack.
ROLE CONFLICT Sam performs "servant," "friend," "surrogate father," "moral philosopher" simultaneously. When Sam rebukes Hally about his father, Hally punishes him: "You're only a servant here."
STIGMA Hally's father is stigmatised by disability, and Hally has internalised this as shame. "I'm sick and tired of emptying stinking chamberpots." Shame drives displacement onto Sam.
B. Frantz Fanon: Postcolonial Theory
The Wretched of the Earth, 1961
Postcolonial

The colonial world is Manichaean, divided into two absolutely opposed zones: the settler's town (full, lit, stone) and the native town (crouching, dark, hungry). No grey area. The settler is the absolute beginning; the native is defined by lack. Violence is structural.

ConceptApplicationTextual Evidence
Manichaean World Tea room (settler's world) vs. servants' quarters (native's world). Tea room = well-lit, comfortable; quarters = "grey little room with cold cement floor."
Settler as Absolute Beginning Hally cannot conceive of Sam as having independent knowledge. "Tolstoy may have educated his peasants, but I've educated you."
Spatial Exclusion The Whites Only bench = Fanon's frontier made literal and legal. Sam could not sit with Hally. Hally: "too young, too excited to notice." Privilege = invisible frontier.
"Filth" Transfer / The Spit Hally faces his father's disability (family's filth) and spits it onto Sam. Walder: "the contempt he is unable to show his father." The spit is displaced shame.
Should Sam Have Hit Back? Fanon asks: is non-violence a form of complicity? A Black audience member reportedly said Sam "should have kicked Hally's ass a long time ago." Sam's restraint is conditioned by the threat of deportation.
C. G.W.F. Hegel: The Master-Slave Dialectic
Phenomenology of Spirit, 1807
Philosophical

Two consciousnesses meet and each desires to be recognised as fully human and free. One submits. The master requires the slave's recognition, but recognition extorted from a person you have debased is worthless. The master becomes dependent on the slave; the slave, in labour and dignity, becomes freer than the master.

Hegelian MomentThe Irony
The demand "Master Harold" By insisting on Sam's submission, Hally achieves the opposite of freedom. He becomes dependent on forced deference, which means nothing. Sam's dignity is unbroken.
Sam's challenge at the bench An invitation to genuine mutual recognition, the Hegelian resolution. Hally can choose to walk away. The future (Fugard's real life) suggests this choice is possible.
The final dance Sam and Willie enact a world of mutual recognition, a world without collisions. The slaves dance freely while the master has left, diminished.
D. David V. Urban & Tolstoy: Christian Humanism
Urban, Renascence, 2010 · Tolstoy, The Death of Ivan Ilych, 1886
Comparative / Humanist

The central irony: Hally claims to admire Tolstoy for his egalitarianism and Christian pacifism, yet embodies their precise opposite. Sam actually exemplifies Tolstoyan values in his patient, non-violent service. Urban's parallel: Sam is Gerasim (the peasant servant in Ivan Ilych who tends to the dying man without embarrassment); Hally is Ivan Ilych, the man who is "ill" in the moral sense.

The chamberpot: Gerasim empties it without shame, with love. Hally is disgusted. Sam's patience with Hally, which excuses his cruelty as illness, mirrors Gerasim's compassionate service exactly. Hally's claim to Tolstoy is devastatingly ironic: he is the subject of Tolstoy's moral lesson.

E. Sigmund Freud: Displacement & the Unconscious
The Interpretation of Dreams, 1899 and broader psychoanalytic theory
Psychoanalytic

Displacement: emotional energy directed at one object is redirected onto a safer target. Hally cannot confront his feelings about his father, which mix shame with love and disgust. He displaces this unresolvable emotion onto Sam.

The spit is really directed at the father whose body Hally empties chamberpots from, whose drunkenness destroys the family home, whose existence Hally is both ashamed of and unable to abandon. Sam becomes the safe target for the rage Hally cannot express to his father. Apartheid provides the ideological permission for this displacement.

F. David Hoegberg: The Shakespeare Parallel
Comparative Drama, 1996 · Henry IV, Parts 1 & 2
Comparative

Hally → Hal (Prince Henry): both are young men choosing between a lower-class mentor (Sam / Falstaff) and the dominant ideology of their class. Both ultimately betray the mentor who has given them their humanity. Hoegberg reads the "nigger's arse" joke as Hally's "vile participation", the moment Prince Hal joins his father's court by publicly humiliating Falstaff.

Ballroom dancing descends from African rhythms (Hoegberg) and is a site of cultural reclamation. Shakespeare's "Hal" becomes Fugard's "Hally": the same tragedy of choosing class loyalty over genuine human love.

Section 07

Annotated Key Quotations

"A world in which accidents don't happen."
Sam, describing the ballroom dance competition
Central utopian image. The dance floor works as political allegory, where no collisions = no apartheid. Symbolic of the ideal society Sam is trying to build in Hally's mind. Also foreshadows the play's "collision" when Hally spits. The conditional tense ("a world in which") signals that this remains an aspiration for now.
"Tolstoy may have educated his peasants, but I've educated you."
Hally to Sam
Urban's central irony: Hally claims the Tolstoyan mantle while enacting its precise opposite. Sam is his educator. Fanon: Hally positions himself as the "absolute beginning" of Sam's intellectual life, the settler who believes the native has no prior existence. The irony is devastating: the play is Fugard's tribute to Sam's moral education of him.
"You're only a servant here, and don't forget it."
Hally to Sam
Goffman: emergency reassertion of front-stage roles when back-stage intimacy has gone too far. Hegel: Hally demands the master-slave structure to restore his threatened self-image. The word "only" performs the psychopathology of apartheid, reducing a full human being to a social function. Note also: this comes immediately after Sam crosses a role boundary by rebuking Hally about his father.
"It's a case of illness with you, sir."
Gerasim to Ivan Ilych (Tolstoy), echoed in Sam's behaviour toward Hally
Urban's parallel: Sam's patience with Hally mirrors Gerasim's compassionate service to the dying Ivan Ilych. Hally's cruelty is excused as "illness", both the illness of being a child of an alcoholic and the "psychopathology of apartheid" (Durbach). Sam forgives Hally because he understands the cause of the act.
"You were too young, too excited to notice then. But not anymore."
Sam, revealing the truth about the Whites Only bench
The educational climax. Sam withdraws the protective screen of ignorance he has maintained for Hally's sake. Now Hally knows, and is responsible. Ignorance can no longer be the excuse. Goffman: Sam steps wholly out of the servant performance. This is Sam's backstage self speaking, finally, without the waiter's coat. The moment is an act of love and of moral demand.
"You don't have to sit up there by yourself. You know what that bench means now, and you can leave it any time you choose."
Sam's final words to Hally
The play's moral crux. The bench = apartheid privilege. The choice = Hally's. Hegel: the invitation to genuine mutual recognition. The future (Fugard's own life story) suggests this choice is possible. Fugard builds a hope into the ending that is conditional and hard-won. "Any time you choose" asserts free will in the face of a deterministic ideology.
"I felt for that little boy... Master Harold. I felt for him."
Sam, about the kite incident
The kite story reveals Sam as a genuine father figure motivated by love. Sam's use of "Master Harold" here is devastating. It is both the title Hally demanded earlier (as a weapon) and the name of the child Sam loved and pitied. The title collapses its two meanings: oppressive social hierarchy and a father's tender grief.
Section 08

Essay Frameworks & IB Guidance

Paper 2 and the HL Essay require a clear, argued claim using textual evidence and at least one critical perspective, named explicitly.

Sample Question: How does Fugard use the tea room setting to dramatise the power structures of apartheid South Africa?
1

Thesis: The St. George's Park Tea Room functions as a microcosm of apartheid society, encoding racial hierarchy in space, objects, and body positions before a word is spoken. Goffman's front/back stage framework reveals this spatial politics with precision.

2

Para 1: The opening stage picture puts Willie on his knees and Sam reading. Goffman's "setting" (physical backdrop) communicates hierarchy before dialogue. Fugard's stage directions are a deliberate political statement.

3

Para 2: The front/back stage division between tea room and servants' quarters. Fanon's two zones made architectural. Hally's childhood visits to the servants' quarters = crossing the colonial frontier; he is "punished" for it.

4

Para 3: Through the telephone, the outside world (apartheid) reaches into the private space and destabilises it. The Whites Only bench brings the exterior legal order into the room symbolically.

5

Para 4: In the final dance the set becomes utopian. Sam and Willie transform the same space into the world without collisions. Form enacts the thematic argument: space can be reimagined.

Final Checklist for IB Responses

  • Use the author's name in your thesis: "Fugard…" not "the play…"
  • Name your critical lens explicitly: "Through a Fanonian lens…" or "Applying Goffman's concept of front/back stage…"
  • Quote precisely, including page/act references where possible
  • Every analysis point must connect technique → effect → meaning
  • For Paper 2: make the comparison do analytical work in every paragraph
  • For the IO: identify a global issue that is genuinely transnational (race, education, power, identity)
  • Avoid plot summary: state what Fugard does, then why it matters
  • End with the broader significance: what does the text say about the human condition?
