Examination Format

What is Paper 1?

Paper 1 presents you with one or two previously unseen literary texts. You write a literary commentary guided by a question. The examiner wants to see how carefully and perceptively you read, and how well you write about literary choices.

Higher Level

Duration 2 hours 15 minutes
Texts given Two unseen texts
You write Commentary on one
Text types Poetry + Prose (or both prose)
Guiding question Provided for each text
Weighting 35% of final grade

Standard Level

Duration 1 hour 15 minutes
Texts given Two unseen texts
You write Commentary on one
Text types Poetry + Prose (or both prose)
Guiding question Provided for each text
Weighting 35% of final grade
A

Understanding & Interpretation

How well do you understand what the text is saying? Do you interpret meaning beyond the surface?

B

Appreciation of Writer's Choices

Do you identify and analyse specific literary techniques, and explain their effect on meaning?

C

Organisation & Development

Is your commentary logically structured? Do ideas develop and connect across paragraphs?

D

Language

Is your register formal, precise, and appropriate? Do you use literary terminology correctly?

About the Guiding Question

Each text comes with a guiding question, for example "How does the poet use imagery to explore the theme of loss?" Your commentary must address this question, but it should not be your only focus. A strong commentary explores the text as a whole and allows the guiding question to shape your analysis.

Practice Texts

Read, Annotate, Respond

Choose a text below and practice your commentary. Each text includes a guiding question and annotation hints. Work without notes first, then use the hints to deepen your analysis.

Poetry "Neutral Tones" Thomas Hardy, 1867
We stood by a pond that winter day,
And the sun was white, as though chidden of God,
And a few leaves lay on the starving sod;
    They had fallen from an ash, and were gray.
Your eyes on me were as eyes that rove
Over tedious riddles of years ago;
And some words played between us to and fro
    On which lost the more by our love.
The smile on your mouth was the deadest thing
Alive enough to have strength to die;
And a grin of bitterness swept thereby
    Like an ominous bird a-wing.
Since then, keen lessons that love deceives,
And wrings with wrong, have shaped to me
Your face, and the God-curst sun, and a tree,
    And a pond edged with grayish leaves.
Guiding Question

How does Hardy use imagery and setting to explore the experience of a love that has faded?

Annotation Hints: What to Look For
Notice the colour palette throughout the poem. What does the repeated use of white, gray, and ashen tones suggest about the emotional atmosphere?
Consider the word "chidden" (stanza 1). God scolds the sun. What does it mean when the natural world itself is under divine reproach?
In stanza 2, the eyes "rove / Over tedious riddles". What does the simile reveal about the state of this relationship?
Examine the paradox "the deadest thing / Alive enough to have strength to die" (stanza 3). How does Hardy compress emotional contradiction into language?
The poem begins and ends near the same pond. What does this circular structure suggest about memory and trauma?
Notice the stanza form: four stanzas, each four lines, with an indented fourth line. How does this regularity contrast with the emotional instability the poem describes?
Prose From The Awakening Kate Chopin, 1899

The voice of the sea is seductive; never ceasing, whispering, clamoring, murmuring, inviting the soul to wander for a spell in abysses of solitude; to lose itself in mazes of inward contemplation. The voice of the sea speaks to the soul. The touch of the sea is sensuous, enfolding the body in its soft, close embrace.

She was becoming herself and daily casting aside that fictitious self which we assume like a garment with which to appear before the world. Edna spent whole days on her private terrace, reading, day-dreaming. She had resolved never again to belong to another than herself.

The water of the Gulf stretched out before her, gleaming with the million lights of the sun. The voice of the sea spoke in gentle, insistent tones. There was no living thing in sight except a bird with a broken wing that was beating the air above, reeling, fluttering, circling disabled down, down to the water.

Guiding Question

How does Chopin use language, imagery, and symbolism to present Edna's relationship with identity and freedom?

Annotation Hints: What to Look For
Chopin uses an extended personification of the sea as a speaking, touching presence. What emotional and psychological role does the sea play in the passage?
Notice the list of participial phrases: "whispering, clamoring, murmuring." How does this accumulation of sound words create a sensory and emotional effect?
Examine the simile of the "fictitious self … like a garment". What assumptions about identity and performance does it contain?
Consider the shift from second paragraph to third: from interior (selfhood) to exterior (seascape). How does Chopin use spatial movement to reflect psychological movement?
The bird with the broken wing appears at the end. It is "reeling, fluttering, circling disabled down, down." What might this image symbolise, given the passage's context?
Look at sentence length and rhythm. How does Chopin use long, flowing sentences in the first paragraph and shorter ones later? What effect does this create?
Poetry "Home is so Sad" Philip Larkin, 1958
Home is so sad. It stays as it was left,
Shaped to the comfort of the last to go
As if to win them back. The books, the china,
The pictures and the cutlery, the fender,
The tiny family photographs, the lamp —
All that has served its purpose fails to charm.
It has no heart to put aside the theft
Of what the willing soul once made a home
Of what it treasured. Rather, it turns pathetic:
You can see how it was: look at the photographs.
Guiding Question

How does Larkin use personification, structure, and specific detail to explore the sadness of an empty home?

Annotation Hints: What to Look For
The poem opens with a flat, declarative statement: "Home is so sad." How does the simplicity of this opening affect the reader? What tone does it set?
The house is personified as waiting: "as if to win them back." Explore how the inanimate is given desire and intention. What emotional effect does this create?
Notice the list of domestic objects in stanza 1: books, china, photographs, lamp. Why does Larkin enumerate these items? What do they collectively represent?
The word "theft" in stanza 2 is unexpected. What has been stolen, and by whom? Consider its implications for how Larkin views time and loss.
The poem is two five-line stanzas (a decima split). How does the form contain grief, almost compartmentalising it? Is the structure itself a kind of restraint?
The final line: "You can see how it was: look at the photographs." The speaker suddenly addresses "you." What is the effect of this direct address at the poem's close?

Step-by-Step Method

How to Approach Paper 1

A strong commentary is produced by a systematic method practised until it becomes automatic. Follow these steps in the examination room.

01
5 min

Choose Your Text

Read both texts and their guiding questions once through. Do not annotate yet. Ask yourself: Which text gives me more to say? Where do I feel genuine curiosity? Choose with your head. A shorter poem is not always easier than a prose extract.

02
10 min

Read Actively and Annotate

Read your chosen text three times. On the first read, get a sense of the whole. On the second, begin marking:

  • Underline or circle key words, images, repeated words
  • Note tone shifts, structural divisions, turning points
  • Label any techniques you recognise (metaphor, enjambment, irony…)
  • Write brief margin notes: what effect does this create?
  • On the third read, look for patterns across the whole text
03
5 min

Plan Your Commentary

Write a brief outline of the three key points you will develop. Each point should combine a literary technique with a specific effect on meaning or theme. Resist the urge to write about every technique you found. Depth beats breadth every time.

  • Introduction: overall impression, context, what the text "does"
  • Body Point 1: usually the opening. How does the text establish itself?
  • Body Point 2: the central concern, with key technique and effect
  • Body Point 3: the turn or resolution. How does meaning develop or shift?
  • Conclusion: the text as a whole. What does it finally achieve?
04
10 min

Write a Strong Introduction

Your introduction should orient the reader, address the guiding question directly, and signal the direction of your argument. Avoid plot summary. Avoid beginning with "In this poem/passage…" Aim for a sentence that makes a genuine claim.

  • Name the author, title (if known), form, and approximate period or context
  • State the central subject or speaker's situation in one sentence
  • Make a clear claim about the text's dominant mood, technique, or effect
  • Signal the focus of your commentary (link to guiding question)
05
50 min

Write Your Body Paragraphs

Each body paragraph should develop one key idea. Embed quotations, and analyse every one you use. For every technique you name, explain why the writer made that choice and what effect it creates on the reader. Connect each paragraph to the text's larger meaning.

  • Open each paragraph with a clear, analytical claim
  • Embed a short, specific quotation as evidence
  • Analyse choices at the level of individual words
  • Link to theme, tone, or the guiding question
  • Move through the text in order where possible
06
5 min

Write Your Conclusion

Bring together the threads of your commentary into a synthesis. Ask yourself: What does this text ultimately achieve? What does it leave the reader with? A strong conclusion often returns to the opening image or phrase and shows how your analysis has illuminated it.

07
5 min

Review and Refine

Read your commentary once through. Check that each paragraph makes an analytical claim. Correct any errors in quotation. Ensure your response addresses the guiding question. Cut or condense anything that does not move your argument forward.

Analytical Question Bank

Questions to Ask the Text

Before you write a single sentence of your commentary, interrogate the text. Use these questions to open up areas of analysis. You will not answer all of them; they are prompts to find what is worth writing about.

Speaker, Situation & Context
Who is speaking? What is their situation? Are they reliable?
Who is the implied audience? Are they addressed directly?
What has just happened, or is about to happen?
What does the speaker want, consciously or unconsciously?
What is the relationship between speaker and any other figures?
What does the speaker know that the reader may not (or vice versa)?
Imagery & Language
What is the dominant image or metaphor? What does it suggest?
Are there any unexpected or striking word choices? Why might the writer have chosen them?
What sensory language is present (visual, auditory, tactile, olfactory)?
Are there patterns of imagery (e.g. light and dark, water, animals)?
Where does the language become most dense, compressed, or ambiguous?
Are there any words with double meanings or unresolved ambiguity?
Structure & Form
Where is the turn or shift in the text? What changes at that moment?
How does the text begin and end? Is there a contrast or a return?
How does sentence or line length vary, and what effect does this create?
Is there a regularity of form (sonnet, stanza, paragraph length)? Is it broken anywhere?
Where does enjambment, caesura, or punctuation create unexpected pauses or rushes?
Does the structure mirror or contradict the content?
Tone & Atmosphere
What is the emotional register of the opening? Does it change?
Where is the tone most controlled or restrained? Most raw or intense?
Is there irony, ambivalence, or emotional complexity the speaker may not acknowledge?
What mood does the setting or physical environment create?
Does the speaker's tone match what they are describing, or is there a gap?
Theme & Meaning
What is the text "about" beneath its literal surface?
What large human experience does this text engage (love, loss, time, power, identity)?
Does the text offer any resolution, or does it remain deliberately open?
What does the text suggest about how the world works, or how it should work?
What is the reader left feeling or thinking at the end? Is that effect intentional?
The Writer's Craft
Why did the writer choose this form over another? Why a poem rather than prose?
Which single choice (one word, one line break, one structural decision) is the most significant?
What has the writer chosen NOT to say? What is absent?
If one word were changed, how would the meaning shift?
How does the ending reflect back on everything that came before?

5-Question Reading Frame: Answer These Before You Write
1

What is happening in this text?

Write one or two sentences describing the literal situation: who is present, where, what is occurring. Do not interpret yet; just describe what you see.

2

What feeling does this text give you?

Name the dominant emotion or atmosphere: melancholy, unease, longing, joy, irony… Then ask: which specific words or images create that feeling?

3

What is the most interesting or unusual moment?

Identify one moment (a line, a word, a structural choice) that surprises you or seems especially important. This is often your best analytical entry point.

4

What does the text want the reader to feel or understand?

Think about the writer's intention. What experience are they crafting for you? Is the text trying to move you, disturb you, comfort you, challenge you?

5

How does this text connect to the guiding question?

Re-read the guiding question. Which of the techniques or images you identified are most relevant to answering it? Choose three to build your paragraphs around.

Body Paragraph 2: The Central Concern

The most important technique or image in the text. This paragraph should contain your deepest, most specific analysis. Quote precisely and examine individual word choices.

≈ 15 min to write

Body Paragraph 3: The Turn or Development

Where does meaning shift or complicate? This might be the poem's volta, a structural break, or a moment where the speaker's certainty falters. What changes, and why does it matter?

≈ 15 min to write

Conclusion

What does the text ultimately achieve? Synthesise your argument. Return to the guiding question. What understanding of the human experience does this text leave the reader with?
